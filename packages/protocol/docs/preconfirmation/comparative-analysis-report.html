Executive verdict. The redesign is a meaningful protocol-security improvement over today's whitelist on the axes it explicitly targets: it replaces reputation-only enforcement with objective faults and collateral, gives the oldest gap a permissionless recovery path, makes forced inclusions structural, and defines a far more rigorous state machine. Those are real gains.

It is not, however, an across-the-board upgrade. The current whitelist is centralized but small, understandable, inexpensive, and already integrated with two client stacks. v9 exchanges that simplicity for a new auction, a multi-tranche dual-asset collateral system, per-epoch commitments and proofs, an append-only evidence spine, L2-to-L1 safety adjudication, forced-item lifecycle state, and several outage and cancellation modes. Phase A would retain allowlisted participation while already paying most of this complexity cost.

Recommendation: accept PR #22034 as a strong design and decision record, but do not treat merge as authorization for a full replacement. Implement it incrementally behind a guarded Phase-A deployment, retain a tested whitelist rollback/fallback until the structural gates and empirical benchmarks close, and narrow several claims identified below before freezing the mechanism.

3 · What v9 genuinely improves

01 · TRUST

Open-entry path

Phase B removes both validator opt-in and the frozen URC dependency. This is a credible path beyond governance-selected operators.

02 · ACCOUNTABILITY

Objective faults

Missed duties become computed facts rather than discretionary ejector judgments, and withdrawal checks cannot ignore an unpoked fault.

03 · RECOVERY

Oldest-gap lane

A single openEpoch, descendant tolling, and permissionless recovery directly address serial handover and backlog attacks.

04 · CENSORSHIP

Forced minimum

A forced snapshot makes empty invalid and gives users a protocol path that does not depend on the seat's cooperation.

  • Clearer security model. Nine normative invariants, stable fault identities, precommitted payees, a forced-item nullifier, and an append-only evidence history are far more reviewable than today's reputation-and-ejection model.
  • Better failure isolation. The degradation ladder attacks the oldest failing epoch before escalating to recovery-only mode or a systemic freeze. A single holder should not obtain a global pause lever.
  • Oracle-free denomination where it matters. ETH safety collateral is a sensible answer to an ETH-denominated equivocation/MEV gain; keeping the theft deterrent in the same numéraire removes a major reflexive-collateral failure.
  • Outcome independence from L1 packing. Committing epoch-native derivation inputs rather than deriving block identity from eventual L1 inclusion is the right architectural direction for precomputable proofs and recovery.
  • Recovery incentives are attached to faults. The faulter's own senior recovery tranche pays first, while copied witnesses cannot redirect precommitted rewards. This is substantially stronger than hoping an ejector or successor acts altruistically.
  • The checker is useful. The model and eleven mutation tests demonstrate that the written state machine has nontrivial internal discipline; previous reviews caused real structural improvements rather than cosmetic edits.

4 · What the whitelist still does better

Centralized does not mean valueless. The whitelist's main benefit is legibility: a small roster, one eligibility check, epoch-randomized rotation, known operational identities, immediate removal, and existing client reconciliation. Its trust assumption is strong but easy to state and monitor.

5 · Findings and residual risks

No new reason to block this documentation-only PR. The report supports merging the design record. The findings below are mechanism-claim corrections or implementation gates that should be resolved before production replacement.

F1HighT_max is a renewal checkpoint, not a bound on actor-level censorship or control

The proposal calls T_max the objective, Sybil-proof idleness/censorship bound, then correctly admits that a well-capitalized incumbent may keep re-winning. Both statements cannot mean the same notion of “bound.” T_max bounds one tenure ID's uninterrupted reservation and forces recurring price discovery. It does not bound how long the same economic actor—or fresh identities it controls—can retain the seat.

Forced inclusion separately bounds absolute transaction censorship for users who can pay its fee. That protection should carry the censorship claim. Normal-lane denial, ordering power, and sustained application-level discrimination remain possible for a repeatedly winning bidder.

Required clarification: rename T_max as a tenure-renewal/repricing bound; define censorship liveness from the forced-queue SLA; and add explicit concentration goals if operator diversity is a protocol objective.

F2HighThe cancellation predicate is either unobservable or leaves a seal-versus-cancel ordering choice

Section 6.7 says cancellation is reached only when “no valid seal is possible” for H_cancel, while also rejecting an impossible data-availability oracle. L1 can observe that no seal was accepted; it cannot generally prove that no prover or data holder could produce one. The current model makes this ambiguity concrete: a CONTENT seal remains enabled outside an outage while cancellation also becomes enabled after the abstract horizon. L1 transaction ordering then chooses whether content survives, weakening the sender/order-independent outcome claim.

Required clarification: make eligibility purely mechanical. For example, accept seals only before a hard expiry and cancellation only at/after it, with no overlap; or define an explicit attested-outage branch with precise priority. State plainly that unsealed content expires even if a late proof privately existed.

F3HighOutage-robust epoch progress is not forced-message or application liveness

During a permanent proving outage, proof-free cancellation can advance openEpoch, but discretionary content cannot finalize and forced snapshots are re-queued rather than executed. The checker therefore proves a valuable control-plane property—no state-machine deadlock—not that user transactions or bridge messages continue settling.

This distinction matters when comparing against the current solution: empty/cancelled epoch progress may keep bookkeeping alive while application throughput and bridge settlement remain unavailable indefinitely.

Required clarification: publish separate guarantees for (1) epoch-counter progress, (2) forced-queue settlement, (3) discretionary transaction throughput, and (4) bridge completion. Do not summarize all four as “the chain keeps moving.”

F4HighA “hard” preconfirmation needs an exposure model broader than local one-epoch MEV

The ETH safety tranche is sized to exceed one epoch's extractable MEV, and user restitution is out of scope. A conflicting preconfirmation may create profit or harm outside local MEV: cross-domain arbitrage, external-market positions, application credit, or a user's reliance on the promise. Terminating after the first certificate limits repeated protocol faults, but it does not cap the economic value that users may place behind one promise.

The whitelist partially substitutes known-operator reputation for this missing model. An open auction removes that social identity assumption, making the exact guarantee more—not less—important.

Required clarification: define what “hard” guarantees, cap or meter aggregate signed exposure, and specify whether the bond is deterrence-only or expected compensation. Avoid implying that an ETH-denominated slash automatically makes users whole.

F5HighA systemic proving outage should not automatically charge an honest tenure for the cancellation cascade

The same cancellation floor covers attributable data loss and a permanent protocol-wide proving outage, while the causing tenure's recovery tranche is described as paying the cascade. The model follows that rule by charging the owner whenever cancellation fires. In the systemic-outage case, a holder may have published every byte and still be unable to obtain a proof. Charging it as the “causing” tenure converts a protocol failure into an operator loss and may drain honest collateral across consecutive epochs.

Required clarification: distinguish attributable holder faults from attested systemic failures. Socialize or treasury-fund genuinely systemic cancellation work, preserve honest tenure balances, and specify how certificates are forgiven without enabling a holder to self-label ordinary withholding as an outage.

F6Medium–HighPermissionless admission and decentralized sequencing are different properties

The redesign improves entry rights but may reduce day-to-day operator rotation. A standing highest-bidder seat rewards capital, realized-MEV information, builder relationships, and proof infrastructure. T_max creates auctions, not guaranteed turnover. Forced inclusion limits absolute censorship but does not remove ordering power, extraction, latency discrimination, or private order-flow concentration.

Recommendation: decide explicitly whether Taiko optimizes for open contestability, operator diversity, or both. Track seat-share concentration, consecutive epochs, bid competitiveness, and forced-path usage as graduation metrics.

F7Medium–HighThe normal path becomes more expensive and operationally selective

v9 requires continuous publication, a boundary commitment, a proof-carrying seal, deadline monitoring, multiple collateral tranches, and sufficient prover capacity. It also retains per-epoch records on L1 and leaves record compaction/storage accounting open. These costs may be justified, but they work against the “anyone can become the preconfer” narrative in practice.

Recommendation: benchmark end-to-end per-epoch gas/blob cost, worst-case storage growth, proof p50/p95/p99 and retry latency, minimum viable collateral, and the number of independent operators able to meet them before selecting parameters.

F8MediumTAIKO-denominated liveness deterrence remains weakest for the cheapest repeatable fault

The proposal already concedes that a missed empty commit creates only a small ETH recovery charge. Its binding costs are the TAIKO burn, termination, re-admission funding, delay, and auction price. During a TAIKO drawdown or thin auction, anonymous fresh tenures may make that campaign cheaper than intended.

Recommendation: test the cheapest-fault/re-entry loop under low competition and stressed token prices. Phase-B graduation should require an empirical lower bound on re-entry cost, not only a nominal L_live amount.

F9MediumPhase A risks paying nearly all complexity costs before delivering permissionlessness

Phase A keeps auction and fallback participation allowlisted, and its anarchy endgame is DAO-recoverable. It therefore retains the current system's central trust assumption while adding most of the new mechanism and migration surface. This may be appropriate as training wheels, but only if the phase is short, measurable, reversible, and has an objective exit.

Recommendation: make Phase A a bounded pilot with a sunset/review date, explicit rollback conditions, and published Phase-B thresholds. Do not let “training wheels” become the permanent operating mode.

8 · Recommended adoption path

GATE 1

Freeze semantics

Resolve all Phase-A structural items, especially cancellation priority, reorg rewind, verdict transport, forced nullifiers, exact derivation inputs, and outage attribution.

GATE 2

Prototype & measure

Build a non-production vertical slice and publish gas, storage, proving tail latency, bond, and client-migration results.

GATE 3

Shadow Phase A

Run the auction and fault accounting without making it the sole live proposer gate; compare assignments and simulate failures against production observations.

GATE 4

Guarded activation

Enable allowlisted Phase A with caps, a tested rollback, and the current whitelist retained as an emergency migration fallback—not as a silent permanent dependency.

Minimum go/no-go evidence before replacing the whitelist

  • Semantic closure: every §13-S Phase-A item has executable rules and adversarial tests; F1–F5 above are dispositioned.
  • Proof reliability: measured p99 plus congestion/reorg retry budget remains comfortably below S=4, with at least two independent proving implementations/operators.
  • Economic solvency: recovery and cascade reserves cover modeled worst cases without cross-tenure spill; cheapest-fault Sybil loops are uneconomic under stressed assumptions.
  • User guarantee: “soft,” “hard,” forced, anarchy, and outage behavior are specified in user-facing terms, including what is not compensated.
  • Cost: L1 storage growth and normal/recovery gas are benchmarked at p50 and stressed base/blob fees; compaction is designed before the append-only spine launches.
  • Migration safety: Go/Rust/circuit cross-vectors cover all current confirmed-boundary invariants plus the new epoch identity and recovery modes.
  • Phase-B exit: objective graduation thresholds include operator count, bid competition, seat concentration, forced-path affordability, prover diversity, and incident-free runtime.

Bottom line. The current whitelist is a centralized operational system; v9 is an ambitious cryptoeconomic protocol. v9 is clearly better if its goal is objective accountability and eventual open contestability. The whitelist remains better if the immediate priority is simplicity, rotation among known operators, predictable operations, and low migration risk. The right decision is staged replacement with evidence gates—not a binary “new design good, current design bad” judgment.
