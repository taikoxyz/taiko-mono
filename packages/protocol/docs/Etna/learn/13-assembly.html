Lesson 13: final assembly

Prerequisites: every lesson. Design page: Liveness & safety.

Goal

Build the protocol from its blocks in your head, run six scenarios through it, and check that you can reproduce the two arguments that hold it together.

Assembly

  1. Time (lesson 1) gives every block a term and every term an L1 snapshot.
  2. Seats (lesson 2) turn the snapshot and a seed into holders, backups and a committee.
  3. Blocks (lesson 3) are signed, self-describing and locally checkable.
  4. Certificates (lesson 4) turn a block into a bonded fact within a second and a locked fact within two.
  5. View changes (lesson 5) answer silence, seal batches, and make withheld blocks worthless.
  6. Landing (lesson 6) carries a certified prefix and a proof to L1 and writes the checkpoint.
  7. Economics and replacement (lesson 7) keep landings coming without a prover role and never void by clock.
  8. Slashing (lesson 8) makes every harmful deviation impossible, worthless or provable.
  9. Anti-monopoly (lesson 9) prices share and makes exclusion impossible.
  10. Forced inclusion (lesson 10) bounds censorship against everyone.
  11. Anchor and migration (lesson 11) bind L2 to true L1 state and reuse the shared contracts.
  12. L1 environment (lesson 12) is tolerated at every slot time and used only optionally.

The two arguments, in your words

Safety: why is no false state root ever finalized?

A landing writes a checkpoint only after two distinct ZK systems accept a journal whose parent root comes from storage, whose blob hashes come from blobhash, whose anchor tip is checked against L1 history, and whose end hash is bound by a 22-of-32 certificate verified on L1. A false root needs two unsound systems, or one plus a colluding quorum after two hours of stalled landing. Reverting a locked block needs 2Q(m) − m provably slashable attesters, 12 at a full committee.

Liveness: why does the chain keep producing and landing blocks?

Silence is answered in 5 s by a view change, a dead term by the next term, a dead committee by the next committee's fallback in 12 s (which L1 accepts only from then, and only with a certificate at its lock), dead provers by a reward ramp that pays anyone with the data, and a vanished registry by dead mode after 75 terms. No rung needs the DAO or a node's clock alone; each is triggered by signed objects or L1 state.

Cross-module quiz

1. Which single object is both the takeover mechanism and the batch seal, and why is that one object rather than two?

The view change. A separate sequencer-signed seal would be a second withholdable object; the committee's lock already says where the batch ends.

2. Name every rule that reads a node's wall clock, and its consequence when the clock is wrong.

The attester's timeout-signing condition and its 3-second staleness policy. A wrong clock signs late or early; validity and the switch decision (a signed VC) are unaffected.

3. What are the three ways a certified block can be reverted, and their costs?

A view change formed without seeing its certificate (a gossip-delay violation, cost 0, at most an attested block, never a locked one); a landed replacement or forced batch after the deadline machinery (an outage past replaceableFrom plus a landed proof; a recorded REPLACE VC voids nothing until the replacement lands, whichever lands first wins, and no penalty falls on anyone, since round 4 removed the abandonment rule); an L1 reorg (outside T1).

4. Why is the forced-inclusion clock the anchored L1 time and not the landing time?

A proof is computed minutes before landing; an entry becoming due in between would let any third party make the proven landing revert. Anchored time is fixed when the block is built and hash-pinned.

5. Which contract addresses change at activation?

None. The Inbox and Anchor proxies get new implementations; the SignalService, Bridge and Vaults on both layers are untouched; new linked libraries and a verifier are deployed but nothing points at them except the new implementations.

6. What does the DAO control after activation, and what happens if it never acts again?

Upgrades, image-id rotation with an overlap, constants by upgrade, and the Anchor's 25 % share. If it never acts: image windows stay open-ended, single-proof mode and replacement keep the chain moving unless every ZK system is broken, parameters freeze.

7. Give the three bins of the accountability argument with one example each.

Impossible (landing uncertified data), worthless (withholding), slashable (equivocation). No rule accepts an unsigned claim.

8. Which limitation would you attack first as a red-teamer, and why?

Any answer with a trace is right; the design's own list is on the limitations page. The iteration reports record what the red team actually found.

Challenge this: the whole design

Pick one of the six scenarios and change one assumption (a slower gossip layer, a 2-second L1 slot, a 40 % cartel, no Frame Transactions). Re-run the scenario and write down the first rule that no longer holds. Compare with the iteration log; if your finding is not there, it is new.
