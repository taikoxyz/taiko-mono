Your route through the concepts

Each arrow means “read this first.” Every lesson gives a problem, a mechanism, a numerical example, attacks and limits, hidden self-check answers and assumptions to challenge. Follow the route once; use the reference later for exact interfaces.

  1. Three meanings of confirmed — distinguish local validity, acceptance and finality.
  2. Known L1 facts — authenticate the context before checking a block.
  3. Exact bytes — give execution and availability promises one meaning.
  4. Public data before the race — remove first disclosure at settlement.
  5. Data and proof together — understand races, failed provers and frame limits.
  6. A queue that proofs can keep up with — freeze work without starving requests.
  7. Voluntary duties — separate service promises from canonical permission.

What changed after comparing the designs

The course now follows the reference's explicit client confirmation report and clock handling, optional exchange of independently verified proof leaves, and combined timing/resource/funding assessment. You meet each addition only after its prerequisites: confirmation evidence in lesson 1, exact proof reuse in lesson 5, queue delay in lesson 6, publication cost in lesson 8 and the complete operating profile in lesson 12.

After the course, read what we learned from the alternative design and challenge the unresolved production profile. These updates preserve the existing canonical rules; they do not turn the historical review result into proof of launch readiness.

How to challenge a claim

Proven identifies a deduction from stated rules and premises. Try to break a step in that deduction. Assumed identifies something the mechanism cannot provide itself; ask what happens when it fails. Open identifies a decision or missing evidence; do not turn it into a guarantee.

A useful objection has a starting state, specific actions, a cost and consequence, and the requirement it contradicts. Keep locally valid, canonically accepted and final separate. A signature is not proof of universal gossip; a proof is not a source of missing bytes; open entry is not a guarantee of a profitable market.
