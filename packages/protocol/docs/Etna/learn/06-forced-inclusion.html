Freeze the mandatory work

Prerequisites: authenticated origins and atomic proof-bearing acceptance.

The problem: censorship versus a moving target

Proven attack: requiring a proof to include every request present at landing lets an attacker append a transaction just before each proof arrives. The proof's target keeps changing. Ignoring new requests forever has the opposite flaw: a producer can continue the chain while starving a user's transaction.

Proven design choice: each accepted parent fixes its child's mandatory work from an authenticated queue snapshot. New transactions wait without invalidating work in progress.

The mechanism: a frozen FIFO prefix

Proven rule: after Etna becomes ACTIVE, anyone can call force(rawTransaction), publishing bytes on L1 and paying its quoted ETH fee. Entries retain their hash, timestamp, L1 block number and fee. They are append-only, never expire and cannot be administrator-voided. Requests are bounded to 2,048 bytes and the eligible decoded gas limit is at most 1,000,000.

Proven definitions: the parent's cursor identifies the next unprocessed request. Its cut is the unique queue tail after its authenticated origin block. Its snapshot time is that origin's timestamp. The child takes exactly the first up to four entries below the cut for which enqueueTime + 120 ≤ snapshotTime. L1 boundary checks authenticate the cut; the prover cannot choose a convenient shorter queue.

Proven execution rule: run the standard EIP-4788/2935 pre-execution calls, then process the required prefix from the first block's ordinary transaction position zero before discretionary transactions. Rejected requests occupy mandatory-request records, not fabricated transaction positions. The proof binds exact bytes, order and outcomes. Malformed, unsupported or state-invalid requests receive deterministic rejection outcomes; valid requests execute, including ones whose application call reverts. Rejected bytes remain public. Forced inclusion promises a proved attempt, not application success.

Worked example: later arrivals stay behind

Assumed example: parent H has cursor 18, cut 25 and snapshot time 1000. Entries 18–22 arrived at 850; entry 23 at 900. Proven arithmetic: 850+120=970, so the child must process 18–21. Entry 22 remains first. Entry 23 is not due in this snapshot because 900+120=1020. Entry 25 arriving during proving cannot alter these facts.

Proven fee example: with 50 pending entries the enqueue quote is 0.002 ETH: 10¹⁵ × (1 + floor(50/50)) wei. Processing credits 0.001 ETH to the proof-bound beneficiary and sinks 0.001 ETH. Each request's fee remains escrowed until processed; paying a fee buys queue position, not a guaranteed execution result.

Proven bootstrap exception: the first Etna segment retains the specified empty mandatory interval and zero-origin bootstrap sentinel in its parent H0. It needs no activation transaction and records the first real authenticated queue snapshot; ordinary rules apply from segment two. New Etna requests cannot enter before ACTIVE. Requests enqueued after activation retain their records and fees through the first segment; old-protocol requests must drain separately before activation. See the migration specification.

Defenses and limits

Proven: later enqueues cannot sabotage a fixed proof, and later arrivals cannot become predecessors of an existing request. Origins cannot remain indefinitely stale while acceptance advances because origin age is bounded to 900 seconds. Once an entry is due and snapshotted, each successor processes it or removes earlier due entries.

Assumed: capable funded provers, accessible data and continuing affordable L1 inclusion sustain progress. Total prover outage halts processing; paid queue flooding still creates backlog. Open: measured throughput and fee economics must justify any wall-clock service promise. The 120-second maturity is not a 120-second inclusion guarantee.

Proven conditional delay bound: let p bound the request's still-unconsumed predecessors, and assume A-LIVE(Δ): accepted successors continue at intervals no greater than Δ seconds with feasible proving, data and funding. Once ordinary snapshots apply, the FIFO argument gives processedAt ≤enqueueTime +1,020 +(1 +ceil((p+1)/4))×Δ. The 1,020 seconds combines request maturity and maximum origin age; the remaining terms allow a fresh snapshot and enough four-request successors.

Assumed backlog example: with 1,000 predecessors and Δ=660 seconds, the bound is 1,020 +252×660 =167,340 seconds, or 46 hours 29 minutes. It is a conservative conditional bound, not a measured delay, an exact attack outcome or a universal short service promise. Filling an empty queue with 1,000 requests without intervening processing costs 10.5 ETH in queue fees, including a 5.25-ETH permanent sink even if the same actor collects every processing credit. It demonstrates paid friction and a serious delay risk together. Requests larger than 2,048 bytes or 1 million gas have no mandatory path in this revision.

Check your understanding

Can request 25 invalidate the child proving requests 18–21?

Proven: no. The parent-frozen cut and time determine that child's work.

A forced call reverts. May the producer skip its execution?

Proven: no. If state-valid, it executes and receives the reverted outcome; application failure differs from transaction invalidity.

Does 120-second maturity promise processing within 120 seconds?

Proven: no. The request also needs a sufficiently fresh snapshot and enough accepted successors to consume its predecessors. An unbounded paid backlog or failed progress assumptions defeat a uniform short wall-clock guarantee.

Challenge this

Assumed: required work fits available proving and L1 resources. Try moving a discretionary nonce-changing transaction before the prefix, selecting a smaller cut, or keeping the origin stale forever. Which exact acceptance check fails?

Proven conditional use: a forced pinCurrentOrigin() selects the origin of its processing block, so waiting in this queue cannot expire a preselected timestamp. A later pinned reveal can use the same queue under the parser and transaction bounds in lesson 10. Application reverts and funding/nonce requirements still apply.
