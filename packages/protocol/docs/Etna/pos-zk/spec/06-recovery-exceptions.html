06 — Withholding, safe halt, restart, and the unselected recovery fallback

This protocol has no history-replacement path. Mode A is selected (04 — architecture decision §5): a legitimately PoS-finalized block is permanent, and when the protocol cannot continue without violating that, it halts. The fallback (Mode B) is specified here in REC-02 and REC-03 but is not selected, and nothing in it may be implemented, advertised or used without the D2 evidence-and-review procedure.

REC-01 — Mode A: no path may invalidate PoS-finalized history No contract function, client rule, timeout, validator rotation, admission rule, governance proposal executed through the ordinary upgrade path, or operational procedure may change the canonical history at or below a height that reached STATUS-04. Concretely: (a) no function exists whose effect is to lower the accepted checkpoint, replace a finalized block hash at a height, or discard finalized state; (b) no admission rule may reject a batch that extends the current L1 checkpoint (L1-04); (c) a certificate is judged under the validator set of its own epoch and stays valid forever (CONS-08), so validator rotation cannot re-judge it; (d) an upgrade must accept every history finalized under the previous rules (GOV-03); (e) no data-first path may be introduced to "work around" an unsettled batch, because that would let an unproven history be treated as settled (L1-02). This rule is the entire content of Mode A. Its violation is not a bug to be mitigated; it changes the protocol's guarantee class from irreversible PoS finality to provisional confirmation, and would require the D2 procedure before it could be described as legitimate. Proven (structural: the property is stated as an absence of mechanisms plus four positive obligations)

Safe halt

HALT-01 — safe halt obligation A correct validator halts — it stops signing and stops advancing its finalized view — rather than choose between two histories or advance on unknown state. It halts when any of the following holds and cannot be resolved by a rule-legal action: (a) it cannot obtain the data for a finalized or locked block (A-CONS-5 failed); (b) it cannot obtain a quorum for a height within the timeout ladder (A-CONS-2 failed); (c) the L1 facts it must consume are not yet Ethereum-final, or its L1 view disagrees with the consensus view (SYS-02); (d) the L1 validator-set commitment for the epoch it is about to enter is absent (CONS-13); (e) the unsettled-depth cap would be exceeded (HALT-03). Halting is a correct outcome, not a failure of the protocol: it is the action that preserves Mode A. A node that halts must keep serving what it has, and must keep its state so it can resume. Halting never authorises discarding finalized history, and never authorises a data-first publication. Failure mode: an implementer, faced with a stuck chain, invents an "unstick" action — which is precisely the Mode B behaviour that D2 requires to be authorised explicitly, not improvised. Proven Assumed (the halt conditions are normative; whether they are met is assumed)
HALT-02 — restart rules After a halt, a correct participant resumes only from the highest finalized block whose data and certificate it can independently reconstruct. It must never adopt an alternative history at a height it has already finalized, and must never treat a timeout during the halt as evidence that a certificate does not exist (STATUS-10). On restart: (a) each validator enforces a signed-message uniqueness check over its own signing history — no message of the same type at the same height and round may be signed twice, including after a restart or a state resync, and a validator that cannot prove it has not already signed for a (height, round, type) must not sign (the amnesia case is treated as a signing fault, not as a fresh start); (b) a validator that resumes must re-verify the epoch and set for the height it is resuming at rather than trusting its cached set; (c) nodes must not accept a replayed historical message as new — every vote carries the epoch and round, and the receiving rules reject messages for heights already finalized; (d) a resumed node's first act is a data and certificate sync, not a proposal. Failure modes addressed: "amnesia" double-signing after a restart, replaying old votes, and a restart that silently adopts a competing branch — the classic way a safe halt becomes an unsafe restart. Proven (the signing-history rule rests on the validator's own durable state, an implementation gate)
HALT-03 — consensus-enforced unsettled-depth cap (backpressure) Define the unsettled depth at a height as the number of L2 blocks between the highest L1-accepted checkpoint and the block being considered. A correct validator must not prevote or precommit a block whose unsettled depth would exceed D_MAX, where D_MAX is derived from the data-retention window and the L2 block rate and is recorded with its derivation in 09. The cap is a consensus rule: it is enforced by validators, not by an operator, and violating it is objectively detectable. When the cap binds, production stops and the chain is in the safe-halt state until provers catch up. The cap must also account for queueing and L1 inclusion delay; D_MAX is a bound on commitment creation, not on proving latency, and a normal proof taking the whole of the D6 envelope (up to 30 minutes, i.e. 900 blocks at 2 s) is not misconduct and not grounds for any recovery action. Failure modes addressed: unbounded growth of unsettled history (which is exactly the state in which an availability failure becomes unrecoverable), and an implementer treating a slow proof as a fault. Proven unmeasured (D_MAX's value depends on measured retention and proving throughput)
HALT-04 — no emergency operator, no DAO rescue, no data-first recovery No emergency key, guardian, DAO vote, or operational procedure may be required to preserve Mode A, and none may be introduced to "resolve" a halt. Specifically: there is no function that a privileged party can call to advance, rewind, or replace the checkpoint; no DAO intervention is permitted for liveness; and no data-first publication may be used to unblock settlement. If progress requires information or participation that no rule-legal action can obtain, the protocol says so and stops. Failure mode: a halt is quietly "fixed" by an operator, which both violates R2 and disguises a liveness limitation as a functioning protocol. Proven (enforced by the migration's removal of privileged levers, MIG-04)

WH-02 — objective evidence versus local suspicion A protocol-level penalty requires objective, self-contained, on-chain verifiable evidence: two signed messages of the same type at the same height and round with different block ids (CONS-11), a signed proposal that omits a due forced inclusion together with the inclusion record proving it was due (FI-02), or a signed message that violates a rule whose inputs are entirely on-chain. Everything else — a message that did not arrive, a height that did not advance, a proof that has not been submitted, a peer that did not answer a request — is local suspicion. Local suspicion may trigger local actions (retry, view change, reputation, peer selection, disclosure) but may never trigger a penalty, a recovery action, or a statement about what exists elsewhere. Failure modes addressed: false accusations, and the more dangerous error of treating a timeout as a fault ("the sequencer was slashed after a landing deadline" is exactly the reasoning this rule forbids). Proven
WH-03 — false positives and false negatives, labelled unmeasured The specification must state, for every detection rule, both error directions: a false positive penalises a correct participant (for example, a validator whose vote was merely delayed being treated as absent), and a false negative lets a faulty participant escape (for example, a one-off selective-delivery attack that leaves no signed conflict). For each rule the specification states which direction is worse and why, and records that the rates are unmeasured with what would measure them (a fault-injection testbed with a modelled adversarial network). No rule may claim a detection rate. Failure mode: an unmeasured detection rate presented as a guarantee, or a design where the only safe path is perfect detection. unmeasured
WH-04 — reveal-later certificate: the analysis across locks, view changes and reconfiguration The protocol must resist the following schedule, which is mandatory in this specification: (1) a proposer selectively distributes a block to a subset; (2) honest validators vote according to their local views; (3) the attacker privately obtains a finality-related certificate; (4) other participants attempt a leader change, a validator change, or a recovery; (5) the attacker reveals the certificate after the alternative path has progressed. The required conclusion is that two conflicting histories cannot both receive the mode's irreversible guarantee. That conclusion rests on the conjunction of three mechanisms, never on signature counting alone:
  • Locks. A validator that precommitted a block at round R is locked on it and will not prevote a conflicting block at that height unless it sees a proof-of-lock-change at a strictly later round (CONS-04). Because a certificate implies more than one third of the voting power is correct and locked, no conflicting commit can form. This is why an unpublished certificate is harmless to safety: it is the lock, not the publication, that protects the decision.
  • View changes. A view change may change the proposer, but it may not unlock a validator that has not seen a later-round proof-of-lock-change. Therefore step (4)'s "alternative path" cannot produce a conflicting commit merely because the original certificate was hidden.
  • Reconfiguration. At an epoch boundary the new set must carry forward the closing epoch's lock (CONS-09), and certificates are epoch-scoped and permanent (CONS-08). A stakeholder that rotates keys or changes its stake cannot thereby escape a lock it established in the previous epoch.
Failure mode: a design in which the alternative path discards the lock, or in which a validator-set change resets locks, in which case the reveal-later schedule succeeds and two irreversible histories can exist without any slashable equivocation. Proven Assumed (premises: A-CONS-1 in every epoch; correct validators enforce CONS-02/CONS-04; the boundary argument of CONS-09, which is the named review target F1)

The authorised fallback, specified but NOT selected

Mode B is not selected and is not part of the active protocol. The rules below exist for three reasons only: to show the authorised fallback is concrete rather than hand-waving; to enumerate the conditions under which a future decision could make it necessary; and to ensure Mode A is not designed in a way that makes the fallback unimplementable. Nothing here may be implemented, deployed, advertised, or used to describe a PoS confirmation as anything other than irreversible under Mode A. Selecting Mode B requires the D2 procedure: a documented specific requirement that Mode A cannot satisfy, the attempted safe-recovery constructions, an argument or counterexample, and independent review — recorded in the decision log.

Mode B is never described as satisfying Mode A's irreversible finality promise. Under Mode B the label is PoS-certified / provisional, and users must be told that.

REC-02 — Mode B: permissionless L1 recovery of unsettled history (not selected) If Mode B were selected, it would be defined by all of the following, which are stated so that the fallback is fully specified rather than sketched.
ElementRule
InvariantRecovery may only replace history strictly above the last Ethereum-finalized L1 checkpoint. Heights at or below it are untouchable by any recovery path. The new checkpoint after recovery is exactly that L1 checkpoint.
Objective triggerOnly one trigger: a settlement stall — no batch extending the current L1 checkpoint has been accepted for T_STALL, where T_STALL is derived as the D6 proof envelope plus witness generation, queueing, L1 inclusion and a margin, and is recorded with its derivation. T_STALL must be strictly larger than the envelope so that a normal slow proof, or one prover failure inside the envelope, can never trigger recovery.
Who may invokeAnyone, permissionlessly, by posting a bond. No DAO, no operator, no validator quorum. The invocation is an L1 transaction containing only the claim and the bond.
Delay and cancellationThe recovery takes effect only after T_RECOVERY_DELAY. During that window it is cancelled by the acceptance of any valid batch extending the current checkpoint. Acceptance is the only cancellation: neither the reveal of a certificate nor of a proof for a discarded height cancels it, because those heights are by construction not yet settled.
Checkpoint and configuration boundaryThe invoker does not choose the new checkpoint, the new validator set, or any configuration value. Recovery restores the L1-accepted checkpoint and re-enters the epoch schedule at that point; the validator set is whatever the L1 staking contract already fixes for that epoch. This is the rule that prevents recovery from becoming a configuration-capture mechanism.
Late certificatesA certificate for a discarded height is void for canonical purposes but remains valid evidence: if the same validator set signed conflicting certificates, the evidence rules of ECON-04 still apply, and a certificate that was revealed late is not thereby excused. Eligibility to invoke recovery must not depend on the absence of unpublished certificates.
Late proofsA proof whose batch lies above the restored checkpoint is void: its predecessor no longer exists. Its data commitment may be re-used only by a new batch proposed against the restored checkpoint.
Transaction replayL2 transactions in discarded blocks are not replayed automatically and are not guaranteed inclusion. Replay protection must hold across the recovery: message authentication, bridge execution and withdrawal claims are keyed by (chain id, domain, height, commitment) so that an effect executed before the rollback cannot be executed twice afterwards, and an effect that never executed can be submitted again. Deposits already executed on L1 must remain credited.
Derived rollback depthAt most D_MAX L2 blocks, because HALT-03 caps the unsettled depth. Conditional: the bound holds only while the cap is enforced; it is not a bound on loss.
Derived rollback durationT_STALL + T_RECOVERY_DELAY + resume time, all terms unmeasured except that T_STALL must exceed the D6 envelope by construction.
User exposureEvery L2 outcome above the checkpoint: inclusions, ordering, balances, bridge messages not yet settled, and any economic position built on them. No universal bound on economic loss exists and none may be claimed; losses are not compensated by protocol penalties (ECON-11).
Obligations it must still satisfyD5 in full (L1-01, L1-03): recovery is invoked by an L1 transaction whose effect does not accept any batch, and no data-first path may be introduced in its name. Mode B changes the guarantee attached to unsettled confirmations; it does not relax the data-and-proof rule.
Why this is specified even though it is not selected: without it, "we preferred Mode A" is unfalsifiable and an implementer facing a halt would improvise — the worst outcome for both safety and honesty. Assumed (conditional on the D2 procedure being satisfied before any use)
REC-03 — the Mode B blocker is recorded, not waived Selection of Mode B is blocked until the following is established, and the blocker is recorded rather than disclosed as a minor caveat: within the stated normal-operation fault and liveness assumptions, an ordinary malicious leader or a sub-threshold coalition must not be able to cheaply trigger a recovery that replaces honestly confirmed history. The analysis must quantify attacker resources, the penalties the attempt incurs, and the rollback exposure it creates; it must separate legitimate failure handling (an actual stall caused by an actual outage) from attacks on the recovery mechanism itself (engineering a stall to force a rollback); and it must address repeated recovery and races with honest continuation. This requirement cannot currently be established on the evidence available. The trigger is a settlement stall, and a settlement stall can be induced by an outage that an attacker may be able to cause or amplify; the delay and cancellation rules reduce but do not obviously eliminate the profitability of "force a stall, then roll back an unfavourable outcome". Because inability to establish this resistance is a blocker rather than a disclosure item, the correct status is: Mode B is specified and not selected, and it may not be selected on the strength of the analysis in this specification alone. Open (blocker; requires a quantified attack-resource analysis plus independent review)
