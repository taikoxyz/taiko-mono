What this design does not promise

The honest edge of everything in this course: what the design is, what it deliberately leaves unsolved, and which numbers do not exist yet.

This is a design, not a system. Nothing described in this course is implemented, deployed, audited or measured. There is no running network, no benchmark, no audit and no model. Every property called proven in the specification is an argument from named premises, not a machine-checked or tested result, and every number is either arithmetic on stated inputs or an unmeasured placeholder. Where this page and the specification disagree, the specification wins.

The idea

A design earns trust by stating its own boundary. This page is that boundary: the guarantees the design does not make, the duties it does not enforce, and the dependencies it cannot remove. None of these is a temporary gap to be read past — each is part of the design as it stands.

Two of them matter more than the rest for anyone sizing exposure. Forced inclusion is not a mechanism in v1: any account may publish a transaction’s data to L1, but publication is permissionless, advances no protocol state and accepts nothing, no rule obliges any proposer to include a published transaction, and excluding one — published or not — is not a breach. What remains absent is the general inclusion list: no queue, no escrow, no fee, and no L1 entry point for an unpublished transaction. A network-level adversary that can isolate a user — or that can censor L1 inclusion of the publication itself — can keep the data off L1 and exclude that user indefinitely, including preventing the user from starting a withdrawal, and the protocol offers no remedy. And a confirmation above the last batch accepted on Ethereum is provisional: nothing at or below the checkpoint is ever rewritten, and v1 has no path that replaces the history above it — a settlement stall halts the chain safely and clearing it needs a future protocol update.

What each guarantee rests on

The honest list

Scope: a design, not an implementation

There is no implementation, no deployment, no benchmark, no audit and no formal model. Statements that a rule is proven mean it has an argument with named premises; they do not mean it has been tested. Every performance, cost, revenue and market quantity of this design is unmeasured. The only measured-looking numbers in the course are Ethereum constants copied from published standards, and even those are properties of Ethereum rather than of this design.

Confirmations are provisional above the last accepted checkpoint

A PoS confirmation is not settlement. A height above the last batch accepted on Ethereum is provisional: the certificate is valid, but no v1 rule makes it permanent, and v1 has no path that replaces it. A settlement stall halts the chain safely: nothing at or below the last accepted checkpoint is ever rewritten, and the recovery generation — the signed field that scopes certificates and locks — never advances, so no height is retired. Ethereum-final checkpoints and accepted batches are the settled part of the chain; value above the checkpoint, or value never messaged, has no L1-provable claim while production is halted, and no protocol penalty compensates an off-chain loss. Correct participants halt rather than choose a branch, and a halt can be permanent when the missing ingredient is data for a block at or below the accepted checkpoint, the validator set, or the stake assumption. Production also backpressures before that point: validators refuse to vote past the unsettled-depth cap, and a violation of that cap is not objectively punishable at L1 — its only enforcement is honest compliance.

Off-chain loss exposure has no universal bound

History above the last accepted checkpoint is unsettled: its inclusions, ordering, balances and bridge messages are provisional, and any economic position built on them is exposed. The design states no universal bound on that loss and offers no compensation: penalties debit an offender’s stake and are not a claims channel. The unsettled depth is bounded by the cap while the cap is enforced, but the cap is not a bound on loss.

A stalled chain has no way forward in v1

The design has no automatic and no permissionless recovery, and no governance-mediated one either: v1 has no recovery path of any kind, and it gives no protocol-level guarantee of liveness after a stall. A settlement stall halts the chain, and clearing it needs a future protocol update whose procedure is not yet specified. Until then the chain stays halted, and a user’s only exit is the withdrawal path that works from the last settled state on Ethereum alone — through a withdrawal root of k distinct-family attestations, which needs a funded proving market and data that is still retrievable (MEM-15(2b)). This is a social-layer assumption, stated as one: nothing in v1 prices the halt with a bond, enforces a bound on it, or lets any party choose a state, a checkpoint or a height above the last accepted checkpoint.

Membership has a liveness gate, and it does not compel participation

Membership in a future set version is renewed on L1: an entry’s last heartbeat must cover the window that version is judged against — the window derived from the L1 block at which the version’s epoch became coverable, or the one immediately before it — or it is simply not selected into it. The design states what that does and does not buy. It does not repair a version already committed: a chain stalled inside an epoch whose roster cannot form quorum is not released by the heartbeat, and no v1 rule draws a replacement set for that epoch. It does not compel participation: a cohort that keeps attesting while withholding its votes keeps its weight and can stall again. It does not punish a lapse: a missed heartbeat costs slots in future versions and never stake, and re-attesting restores the entry at a later version. And it adds its own dependency: the mechanism works only if honest heartbeat transactions are included on L1, so an adversary able to censor them, delay them past their window, or price them out can exclude honest validators from future versions and thereby raise its own share of those versions — falsifier F8. The bounded term within which a signature may be produced before the window it names is falsifier F9. A change to the window length that takes effect while an epoch is in flight can also leave an entry outside the version being evaluated even though it attested on the grid in force when it signed; the design delays no such change to an epoch boundary and freezes no window per epoch, so this case is another stated limit rather than a repaired one. All of these are carried as open limits, not repaired.

A funded reward pool is what keeps settlement moving

Settlement assumes the landing market is funded: that the fee sweep actually credits the reward pool and that the reward available to a lander is not below the cost of landing. If the pool is empty or the reward is below cost, no rational prover lands, the settlement stall arrives with no adversary at all, and it is reached by economics rather than by attack. No v1 path repairs that: a future protocol update could clear the halt but would change no reward, no fee and no subsidy. Clearing a stall is never a remedy for an unfunded reward pool.

Profit made off-chain from unsettled state is outside any protocol bound

Inclusions, ordering and balances above the last accepted checkpoint are provisional. Profit taken from that state outside the protocol — a position closed elsewhere, a withdrawal credited off-chain, MEV extracted around the unsettled range — is outside any protocol bound. No rule measures it, no penalty recovers it, and protocol penalties are not a compensation channel (ECON-11). The losses on the other side of those trades are equally unbounded.

Data retention is an assumption, not an enforced duty

Settlement requires that somebody still holds a batch’s data and the witness needed to prove it. Retention is a liveness assumption: a failure to serve data is not provable on L1 for most classes, so it produces a halt rather than a penalty. No rule may claim retention is enforced, and no reward or bond makes serving data a duty a validator can be slashed for breaching.

There is no inclusion obligation, and no remedy for isolation

The protocol provides no per-transaction inclusion guarantee, and v1 provides no inclusion obligation at all. Any account may publish a transaction’s data to L1, but publication is permissionless, writes a record and nothing else, and accepts nothing: no rule requires a batch to include, resolve or prove that data, so a proposer that excludes a transaction — published or not — is not in breach, and no due set or omission offence exists (LIVE-04). What does not exist is the general inclusion list: no queue of arbitrary transactions, no escrow, no fee and no L1 entry point that can force an unpublished transaction into L2. Proposer selection is weighted and public, so a coalition holding less than a third of voting power is selected for fewer than a third of slots in expectation, and a transaction that reaches honest validators is included by the first honest proposer with room for it — but that last clause is behavioural, not a rule. A network-level adversary able to isolate a user from every honest proposer, or to censor L1 inclusion of the publication itself, can keep the data off L1 and exclude that user’s transactions indefinitely; because a withdrawal is started by an L2 transaction, the same adversary can prevent the user from starting a withdrawal. The protocol offers no remedy. Proposer selection is fully predictable, so buying or coercing the proposer of one specific slot is possible and no rule prices, detects or penalises it.

A settlement stall is not an inclusion remedy. It halts the chain, and v1 has no path that clears it; it cannot put a transaction into a block or compel any proposer to include one, so it does not lift an exclusion. The general inclusion list is planned as a later protocol update; until that update ships, this version has no L1 path by which a user can force an unpublished transaction into L2, and nothing changes that for published data either.

Rewards depend on a funded pool

Validator and prover rewards are ETH, and they can only be paid out of ETH that has actually flowed into the reward pool. L2 execution and priority fees are the funding source: the L2 fee vault is swept across the preserved Bridge into the L1 reward pool on a fixed cadence, so the honest consequences are that rewards lag the settlement pipeline, an empty pool pays nothing, and a bridging failure stops rewards rather than creating a claim on future revenue. What an unfunded schedule does is not quietly shrink: the validator count falls through the objective exit path, or the chain halts. The subsidy, if one is funded, is bounded, expiring and netted out of the same inflow — it is not a second funding source. TAIKO has no mint path today, so paying rewards in new TAIKO would require a token upgrade and is not a standing source. No rule may be conditioned on TAIKO’s price rising, and there is no verified price, float or liquidity data in this design.

Long-range attacks are handled by weak subjectivity

An attacker who once held a third of the stake and has since exited can still produce an alternative history whose signatures verify, because it kept the keys that were committed at that past epoch; once its stake is released it cannot be punished. The defences are that a node never adopts an L2 history conflicting with its L1 checkpoint, and that it refuses to bootstrap from a checkpoint older than a fixed freshness window. That window is the weak-subjectivity trust requirement: a node cannot validate its way out of it, and if no fresh, Ethereum-final checkpoint exists — because of a long halt or a proving outage — a new node must not present the history it syncs as verified, and new validators cannot be admitted until a newer checkpoint exists.

Governance can change the rules

Upgrades are a governance action, and a governance action can rewrite any rule in this design, including the ones this page describes. Malicious or captured governance is outside the protocol’s protection: the staking ledger, the offence catalogue and the reward identity are all rules that an upgrade could replace. The design's protection in ordinary operation is the absence of an emergency operator or an owner-triggered path that moves bonded stake — not an assumption that governance will always behave. Clearing a settlement stall needs a future protocol update, and v1 itself offers no path at all: the halt is disclosed as a fact of the design, not delegated to a party that must act.

No measured numbers, and no compensation

Proving latency, throughput, bandwidth, storage, retention, operator cost, fee revenue, reward schedules, L1 verification gas, evidence windows and the withdrawal delay are all unmeasured, and v1 has no stall-resolution parameter at all. Penalties are not compensation and no universal bound on user loss exists: a penalty debits an offender’s stake, and losses from a broken safety assumption, a key compromise, a proof-system failure, a malicious upgrade or an L1 reorganisation are not covered by any rule.

What this gives you

  • A clear boundary: which claims are arguments, which are arithmetic, and which numbers simply do not exist yet.
  • An explicit statement that a settled checkpoint is never rewritten while everything above it is provisional, and that v1 has no path that replaces it — a settlement stall halts the chain, and clearing it needs a future protocol update. Where nothing can help, a halt can still be permanent.
  • A recovery generation that is narrow by construction: it is a signed field that scopes certificates and locks, v1 never advances it, no state, checkpoint or height can be chosen through it, and no height is retired.
  • A precise statement of the inclusion limit: no inclusion obligation in v1, no general inclusion list, and for unpublished data only statistical resistance, with no remedy against isolation.
  • The dependencies named — retention, funding, weak subjectivity, governance, and the absence of any recovery path — so they can be monitored and priced deliberately.

What it does not give you

  • No implementation, deployment, audit, benchmark or model to point at.
  • No measurement of any performance, cost or economic quantity of this design.
  • No rewrite of settled history, no emergency operator and no degraded mode — but also no compensation for the off-chain losses that provisional history can cause, and no universal bound on them; and no protocol-level guarantee that a stalled chain resumes at all.
  • No per-transaction inclusion guarantee, no inclusion obligation over published data, no general inclusion list, no retention enforcement and no compensation for loss.
  • No liveness gate that compels participation: the L1 heartbeat decides who is in future set versions, and an entry that stops attesting loses its slots there and no stake. A committed version is never re-drawn, and a cohort that keeps attesting while withholding its votes keeps its weight.
  • No protection against a malicious upgrade, or against a proof-system or cryptographic failure.

Check yourself

Settlement stalls for a week. What does the design do about it?

The chain halts safely: nothing at or below the last checkpoint accepted on Ethereum is rewritten, and a withdrawal whose signal is at or below that checkpoint stays executable on Ethereum alone once a withdrawal root of k distinct-family attestations forms over it and the delay runs from the root’s record with no unexpired veto standing. v1 has no path that clears the stall — no automatic, permissionless or governance-mediated route — so clearing it needs a future protocol update whose procedure is not yet specified. If the missing ingredient is data at or below the accepted checkpoint, nothing can help and the halt can be permanent. And if the real cause is an empty or below-cost reward pool, no update that merely clears the halt would fix it: it would change no reward and no fee.

What clears a settlement stall in v1?

Nothing. There is no automatic path, no permissionless path and no governance-mediated path in v1, and no protocol-level guarantee of liveness after a stall. Users can still exit from the last settled state on Ethereum alone through a withdrawal root that k distinct backend families must attest from data that is still retrievable and that an unexpired veto can delay (MEM-15(2b)), but unsettled value above the checkpoint has no L1-provable claim until a later checkpoint covers it. Clearing the stall is left to a future protocol update, whose procedure is not yet specified.

A user is isolated from every honest proposer. Can they withdraw anyway?

Only if the transaction’s data reaches L1, and even then no rule obliges anyone to include it: publication is permissionless and accepts nothing, and no inclusion obligation exists in v1. An adversary that can also keep the data off L1 — by cutting the user off from every honest proposer, or by censoring L1 inclusion of the publication itself — can still prevent the withdrawal from starting, indefinitely, and the protocol offers no remedy. For data that never reaches L1, inclusion resistance is a conditional statistical property, not a guarantee.

Which numbers in this course are measured?

None of this design’s own numbers. Ethereum constants such as the blob gas cost and blob sizes are copied from published standards, and quantities like 900 blocks per thirty minutes are arithmetic on the two-second cadence. Everything else — proving latency, throughput, operator cost, fee revenue, reward rates, evidence and withdrawal delays — is unmeasured.

Assumptions

  • Fewer than one third of the voting power in each epoch is malicious, and enough validators stay online to reach quorum.
  • Somebody retains each batch’s data and witness until its proof lands, and a funded proving market produces the k−1 further attestations a withdrawal root needs from data that is still retrievable.
  • Somebody publishes a transaction’s data to L1 while the blobs are retrievable; publication creates no inclusion obligation either way, so if the data never reaches L1 the protocol has no remedy.
  • Ethereum keeps producing and finalizing blocks, and L1 facts become Ethereum-final at a known depth. Validators post an L1 heartbeat once per window of HEARTBEAT_WINDOW L1 blocks and Ethereum includes those transactions; a lapse costs future slots and not stake, and censoring honest heartbeats can exclude honest validators from future versions (F8).
  • The reward pool is funded by L2 fee revenue or a recorded top-up; otherwise no reward is payable.
  • A fresh, Ethereum-final L1 checkpoint is available to a new node, and governance does not execute an upgrade that breaks these rules.
  • v1 has no stall parameter and no recovery path: a settlement stall halts the chain and clearing it needs a future protocol update, so there is no protocol-level guarantee of liveness after a stall. The exit from the last settled state stays available on Ethereum alone.
