What this design does not promise

The honest edge of everything in this course: what the design is, what it deliberately leaves unsolved, and which numbers do not exist yet.

This is a design, not a system. Nothing described in this course is implemented, deployed, audited or measured. There is no running network, no benchmark, no audit and no model. Every property called proven in the specification is an argument from named premises, not a machine-checked or tested result, and every number is either arithmetic on stated inputs or an unmeasured placeholder. Where this page and the specification disagree, the specification wins.

The idea

A design earns trust by stating its own boundary. This page is that boundary: the guarantees the design does not make, the duties it does not enforce, and the dependencies it cannot remove. None of these is a temporary gap to be read past — each is part of the design as it stands.

Two of them matter more than the rest for anyone sizing exposure. The protocol provides no forced-inclusion path in this version, so a network-level adversary that can isolate a user can exclude that user’s transactions indefinitely — including preventing the user from starting a withdrawal; forced inclusion is planned as a later protocol update, and recovery is not an inclusion remedy. And a confirmation above the last batch accepted on Ethereum is provisional: a bonded, delayed, permissionless recovery can replace that unsettled history — and permanently retires it, so it cannot come back.

What each guarantee rests on

The honest list

Scope: a design, not an implementation

There is no implementation, no deployment, no benchmark, no audit and no formal model. Statements that a rule is proven mean it has an argument with named premises; they do not mean it has been tested. Every performance, cost, revenue and market quantity of this design is unmeasured. The only measured-looking numbers in the course are Ethereum constants copied from published standards, and even those are properties of Ethereum rather than of this design.

Confirmations are provisional above the last accepted checkpoint

A PoS confirmation is not settlement. Every height above the last batch accepted on Ethereum can be replaced by a recovery: a permissionless, bonded, delayed L1 action that discards the unsettled range and resumes from that checkpoint. The discarded heights are permanently retired — never certified or landed again, at any generation, by anyone — so the replaced history cannot come back, and locks at those heights are void. Ethereum-final checkpoints and accepted batches are never touched; a rolled-back L2 transaction is not replayed automatically and must be resubmitted, and off-chain losses are not compensated. Where recovery cannot help — data for a block at or below the accepted checkpoint is gone, the validator set is unavailable, or the stake assumption has failed — correct participants still halt, and a halt can be permanent. Production also backpressures before that point: validators refuse to vote past the unsettled-depth cap, and a violation of that cap is not objectively punishable at L1 — its only enforcement is honest compliance.

Off-chain loss exposure has no universal bound

Recovery replaces inclusions, ordering, balances and bridge messages that were only provisional, and any economic position built on them. The design states no universal bound on that loss and offers no compensation: penalties debit an offender’s stake and are not a claims channel. The rollback depth is bounded by the unsettled-depth cap while the cap is enforced, but the cap is not a bound on loss.

Recovery is a manipulation primitive with a price

Because a settlement stall triggers recovery, an adversary who can cause or prolong a stall may be able to replace unfavourable unsettled history. The defences are the non-refundable bond component paid on every attempt, the delay, cancellation by any valid batch (which sends the whole bond to the protocol treasury rather than back to the invoker), the escalation that counts attempts, and the cooldown after every attempt; within the stated L2 fault model a sub-threshold coalition cannot by itself force the trigger. Completion is not free either: only the refundable balance returns, and the non-refundable component is already gone. The design does not claim attempts are impossible, and it does not bound the off-chain profit of a successful manipulation.

The recovery-safety argument has conditions, and is not yet independently reviewed

The argument that recovery cannot be cheaply abused would fail if L1-level censorship keeps valid batches from landing for the whole delay, if no prover exists at all for the stalled range, or if the recovery parameters are mis-set relative to the measured proving and settlement pipeline. Those are the conditions under which it would fail; the argument is not a proof, and no rate or cost in it is measured. The mechanism has also not yet been independently reviewed, so its safety case stands as an argument from stated premises rather than as a checked result.

Recovery safety depends on a funded reward pool

The resistance argument assumes the landing market is funded: that the fee sweep actually credits the reward pool and that the reward available to a lander is not below the cost of landing. If the pool is empty or the reward is below cost, no rational prover lands, the settlement stall arrives with no adversary at all, and the trigger is reached by economics rather than by attack. Recovery cannot repair that: it restores a checkpoint and retires a range, and it changes no reward, no fee and no subsidy. Recovery is the remedy for a stalled chain, never for an unfunded reward pool.

Profit made off-chain from a rolled-back state is outside any protocol bound

A recovery replaces inclusions, ordering and balances above the restored checkpoint. Profit taken from that state outside the protocol — a position closed elsewhere, a withdrawal credited off-chain, MEV extracted around the discarded range — is outside any protocol bound. No rule measures it, no penalty recovers it, and protocol penalties are not a compensation channel (ECON-11). The losses on the other side of those trades are equally unbounded.

Data retention is an assumption, not an enforced duty

Settlement requires that somebody still holds a batch’s data and the witness needed to prove it. Retention is a liveness assumption: a failure to serve data is not provable on L1 for most classes, so it produces a halt rather than a penalty. No rule may claim retention is enforced, and no reward or bond makes serving data a duty a validator can be slashed for breaching.

No forced inclusion, and no remedy for isolation

The protocol provides no per-transaction inclusion guarantee and no L1 forced-inclusion path. Proposer selection is weighted and public, so a coalition holding less than a third of voting power is selected for fewer than a third of slots in expectation, and a transaction that reaches honest validators is included by the first honest proposer with room for it — but that last clause is behavioural, not a rule: no inclusion duty attaches to a validator, and omission is not an offence. A network-level adversary able to isolate a user from every honest proposer can exclude that user’s transactions indefinitely; because a withdrawal is started by an L2 transaction, the same adversary can prevent the user from starting a withdrawal. The protocol offers no remedy. Proposer selection is fully predictable, so buying or coercing the proposer of one specific slot is possible and no rule prices, detects or penalises it.

Recovery is not an inclusion remedy. It can replace history that was only provisional, but it cannot put a transaction into a block or compel any proposer to include one, so it does not lift an exclusion. Forced inclusion is planned as a later protocol update; until that upgrade ships, this version has no L1 path by which a user can force a transaction into L2.

Rewards depend on a funded pool

Validator and prover rewards are ETH, and they can only be paid out of ETH that has actually flowed into the reward pool. L2 execution and priority fees are the funding source: the L2 fee vault is swept across the preserved Bridge into the L1 reward pool on a fixed cadence, so the honest consequences are that rewards lag the settlement pipeline, an empty pool pays nothing, and a bridging failure stops rewards rather than creating a claim on future revenue. What an unfunded schedule does is not quietly shrink: the validator count falls through the objective exit path, or the chain halts. The subsidy, if one is funded, is bounded, expiring and netted out of the same inflow — it is not a second funding source. TAIKO has no mint path today, so paying rewards in new TAIKO would require a token upgrade and is not a standing source. No rule may be conditioned on TAIKO’s price rising, and there is no verified price, float or liquidity data in this design.

Long-range attacks are handled by weak subjectivity

An attacker who once held a third of the stake and has since exited can still produce an alternative history whose signatures verify, because it kept the keys that were committed at that past epoch; once its stake is released it cannot be punished. The defences are that a node never adopts an L2 history conflicting with its L1 checkpoint, and that it refuses to bootstrap from a checkpoint older than a fixed freshness window. That window is the weak-subjectivity trust requirement: a node cannot validate its way out of it, and if no fresh, Ethereum-final checkpoint exists — because of a long halt or a proving outage — a new node must not present the history it syncs as verified, and new validators cannot be admitted until a newer checkpoint exists.

Governance can change the rules

Upgrades are a governance action, and a governance action can rewrite any rule in this design, including the ones this page describes. Malicious or captured governance is outside the protocol’s protection: the staking ledger, the offence catalogue and the reward identity are all rules that an upgrade could replace. The design's protection in ordinary operation is the absence of an emergency operator or an owner-triggered path that moves bonded stake — not an assumption that governance will always behave.

No measured numbers, and no compensation

Proving latency, throughput, bandwidth, storage, retention, operator cost, fee revenue, reward schedules, L1 verification gas, evidence windows and the withdrawal delay are all unmeasured. Penalties are not compensation and no universal bound on user loss exists: a penalty debits an offender’s stake, and losses from a broken safety assumption, a key compromise, a proof-system failure, a malicious upgrade or an L1 reorganisation are not covered by any rule.

What this gives you

  • A clear boundary: which claims are arguments, which are arithmetic, and which numbers simply do not exist yet.
  • An explicit statement that a settled checkpoint is never rewritten while everything above it is provisional and can be replaced by a bonded, delayed recovery — and that where recovery cannot help, a halt can still be permanent.
  • A priced recovery mechanism: anyone may start it, every attempt pays a non-refundable component whether or not it completes, honest progress cancels it and sends the whole bond to the protocol treasury, and the discarded heights are permanently retired.
  • A precise statement of the inclusion limit: statistical resistance only, with no L1 escape path and no remedy against isolation.
  • The dependencies named — retention, funding, weak subjectivity, governance — so they can be monitored and priced deliberately.

What it does not give you

  • No implementation, deployment, audit, benchmark or model to point at.
  • No measurement of any performance, cost or economic quantity of this design.
  • No rewrite of settled history, no emergency operator and no degraded mode — but also no compensation for the off-chain losses a recovery can cause, and no universal bound on them.
  • No inclusion guarantee, no retention enforcement and no compensation for loss.
  • No protection against a malicious upgrade, or against a proof-system or cryptographic failure.

Check yourself

Settlement stalls for a week. What does the design do about it?

After the stall threshold, anyone may start a recovery by posting a bond; the non-refundable component is paid at once, and every attempt carries a cooldown. It waits for the delay, is cancelled by any valid batch (and the whole bond goes to the treasury), and otherwise replaces everything above the last checkpoint accepted on Ethereum, resumes from there, and permanently retires the discarded heights. A transaction rolled back that way is not replayed automatically. If instead the missing ingredient is data at or below the accepted checkpoint, recovery cannot help and the halt can be permanent. And if the real cause is an empty or below-cost reward pool, recovery does not fix it: it restores a checkpoint and retires a range, but it pays no reward and changes no fee.

A user is isolated from every honest proposer. Can they withdraw anyway?

No. There is no forced-inclusion path, and a withdrawal is started by an L2 transaction, so an adversary that can keep the user’s transactions away from every honest proposer can prevent the withdrawal from starting. The protocol offers no remedy; the honest statement is that inclusion resistance is a conditional statistical property, not a guarantee.

Which numbers in this course are measured?

None of this design’s own numbers. Ethereum constants such as the blob gas cost and blob sizes are copied from published standards, and quantities like 900 blocks per thirty minutes are arithmetic on the two-second cadence. Everything else — proving latency, throughput, operator cost, fee revenue, reward rates, evidence and withdrawal delays — is unmeasured.

Assumptions

  • Fewer than one third of the voting power in each epoch is malicious, and enough validators stay online to reach quorum.
  • Somebody retains each batch’s data and witness until its proof lands.
  • Ethereum keeps producing and finalizing blocks, and L1 facts become Ethereum-final at a known depth.
  • The reward pool is funded by L2 fee revenue or a recorded top-up; otherwise no reward is payable.
  • A fresh, Ethereum-final L1 checkpoint is available to a new node, and governance does not execute an upgrade that breaks these rules.
  • The recovery trigger, bond split, delay, escalation, cooldown and cancellation values are unmeasured, the argument that they cannot be abused rests on stated conditions rather than on measurement, and the mechanism has not yet been independently reviewed.
