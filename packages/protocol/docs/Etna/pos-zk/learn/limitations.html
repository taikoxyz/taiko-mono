What this design does not promise

The honest edge of everything in this course: what the design is, what it deliberately leaves unsolved, and which numbers do not exist yet.

This is a design, not a system. Nothing described in this course is implemented, deployed, audited or measured. There is no running network, no benchmark, no audit and no model. Every property called proven in the specification is an argument from named premises, not a machine-checked or tested result, and every number is either arithmetic on stated inputs or an unmeasured placeholder. Where this page and the specification disagree, the specification wins.

The idea

A design earns trust by stating its own boundary. This page is that boundary: the guarantees the design does not make, the duties it does not enforce, and the dependencies it cannot remove. None of these is a temporary gap to be read past — each is part of the design as it stands.

Two of them matter more than the rest for anyone sizing exposure. Forced inclusion in this version is narrow: any account may publish a transaction’s data to L1, and once the published record is due a batch must resolve it within the capped first-in-first-out prefix of the due set — include it in proven execution, or discharge it as void on objective grounds — and a record that is not proven before its deadline is discarded and can be re-published. That makes exclusion time-bounded, not certain to end in inclusion: it fixes no height by which inclusion must begin, and a backlog drains over successive batches instead of halting the chain. What is still absent is the general inclusion list, so a network-level adversary that can isolate a user — or that can censor L1 inclusion of the publication itself — can keep the data off L1 and exclude that user indefinitely, including preventing the user from starting a withdrawal; the general inclusion list is planned as a later protocol update, and a restart is not an inclusion remedy. And a confirmation above the last batch accepted on Ethereum is provisional: a timelocked, resume-only governance decision can restart the chain from that checkpoint and discard the unsettled history above it.

What each guarantee rests on

The honest list

Scope: a design, not an implementation

There is no implementation, no deployment, no benchmark, no audit and no formal model. Statements that a rule is proven mean it has an argument with named premises; they do not mean it has been tested. Every performance, cost, revenue and market quantity of this design is unmeasured. The only measured-looking numbers in the course are Ethereum constants copied from published standards, and even those are properties of Ethereum rather than of this design.

Confirmations are provisional above the last accepted checkpoint

A PoS confirmation is not settlement. Every height above the last batch accepted on Ethereum can be discarded when the chain restarts: a timelocked, resume-only governance decision resumes from that checkpoint and discards everything above it. Governance chooses no state, checkpoint or height, cannot shorten the timelock once queued, and the decision is void if a batch extending the checkpoint is accepted first. The restart advances the recovery generation, so certificates from the discarded range are void at the acceptance rule, and locks taken there do not bind the new generation. Ethereum-final checkpoints and accepted batches are never touched; a discarded L2 transaction is lost, is not replayed automatically and must be resubmitted, and off-chain losses are not compensated. Where no restart can help — data for a block at or below the accepted checkpoint is gone, the validator set is unavailable, or the stake assumption has failed — correct participants still halt, and a halt can be permanent. Production also backpressures before that point: validators refuse to vote past the unsettled-depth cap, and a violation of that cap is not objectively punishable at L1 — its only enforcement is honest compliance.

Off-chain loss exposure has no universal bound

A restart discards inclusions, ordering, balances and bridge messages that were only provisional, and any economic position built on them. The design states no universal bound on that loss and offers no compensation: penalties debit an offender’s stake and are not a claims channel. The discarded depth is bounded by the unsettled-depth cap while the cap is enforced, but the cap is not a bound on loss.

A stalled chain resumes only if governance acts

The design has no automatic and no permissionless recovery, and it gives no protocol-level guarantee of liveness after a stall. Clearing a stall depends on governance queueing and executing the timelocked, resume-only decision; if governance does not act, the chain stays halted, and a user’s only exit is the withdrawal path that works from the last settled state on Ethereum alone (MEM-15). This is a social-layer trust assumption, stated as one: it is not priced by a bond, not enforced by a rule, and not bounded by any protocol parameter. Governance chooses when the decision executes, but not what it does — the resume point and the discarded range are fixed by L1 state — and progress before execution voids the decision and lets any account cancel the queued entry. A captured or coerced governance can also use the rule opportunistically: it can wait for a genuine stall and let an unfavourable provisional range be discarded. The rule-bound effect, the timelock and the exit window mitigate that; they do not remove it.

A funded reward pool is what keeps settlement moving

Settlement assumes the landing market is funded: that the fee sweep actually credits the reward pool and that the reward available to a lander is not below the cost of landing. If the pool is empty or the reward is below cost, no rational prover lands, the settlement stall arrives with no adversary at all, and the trigger is reached by economics rather than by attack. A restart cannot repair that: it resumes from a checkpoint and discards a range, and it changes no reward, no fee and no subsidy. The restart is a way past a stalled chain, never a remedy for an unfunded reward pool.

Profit made off-chain from a discarded state is outside any protocol bound

A restart discards inclusions, ordering and balances above the restored checkpoint. Profit taken from that state outside the protocol — a position closed elsewhere, a withdrawal credited off-chain, MEV extracted around the discarded range — is outside any protocol bound. No rule measures it, no penalty recovers it, and protocol penalties are not a compensation channel (ECON-11). The losses on the other side of those trades are equally unbounded.

Data retention is an assumption, not an enforced duty

Settlement requires that somebody still holds a batch’s data and the witness needed to prove it. Retention is a liveness assumption: a failure to serve data is not provable on L1 for most classes, so it produces a halt rather than a penalty. No rule may claim retention is enforced, and no reward or bond makes serving data a duty a validator can be slashed for breaching.

Forced inclusion is narrow, and there is no remedy for isolation

The protocol provides no per-transaction inclusion guarantee. What it provides is narrower: any account may publish a transaction’s data to L1, and once the published record is due, a batch must resolve it within the capped first-in-first-out prefix of the due set — include it in proven execution, or discharge it as void on objective grounds — and a record that is not proven before its deadline is discarded and can be re-published (FI-10–FI-14). That is an upper bound on exclusion: it fixes no height by which inclusion must begin, it is never a promise that a particular transaction is included, and because the obligation is a capped prefix a backlog drains over successive batches instead of halting the chain. What does not exist is the general inclusion list: no queue of arbitrary transactions, no escrow, no fee and no L1 entry point that can force an unpublished transaction into L2. Proposer selection is weighted and public, so a coalition holding less than a third of voting power is selected for fewer than a third of slots in expectation, and a transaction that reaches honest validators is included by the first honest proposer with room for it — but that last clause is behavioural, not a rule: no inclusion duty attaches to a validator for a transaction that has not been published and become due, and omitting one is not an offence. A network-level adversary able to isolate a user from every honest proposer, or to censor L1 inclusion of the publication itself, can keep the data off L1 and exclude that user’s transactions indefinitely; because a withdrawal is started by an L2 transaction, the same adversary can prevent the user from starting a withdrawal. The protocol offers no remedy. Proposer selection is fully predictable, so buying or coercing the proposer of one specific slot is possible and no rule prices, detects or penalises it.

A restart is not an inclusion remedy. It can discard history that was only provisional, but it cannot put a transaction into a block or compel any proposer to include one, so it does not lift an exclusion. The general inclusion list is planned as a later protocol update; until that upgrade ships, this version has no L1 path by which a user can force an unpublished transaction into L2, and a publication that L1 refuses to include is outside the narrow rule as well.

Rewards depend on a funded pool

Validator and prover rewards are ETH, and they can only be paid out of ETH that has actually flowed into the reward pool. L2 execution and priority fees are the funding source: the L2 fee vault is swept across the preserved Bridge into the L1 reward pool on a fixed cadence, so the honest consequences are that rewards lag the settlement pipeline, an empty pool pays nothing, and a bridging failure stops rewards rather than creating a claim on future revenue. What an unfunded schedule does is not quietly shrink: the validator count falls through the objective exit path, or the chain halts. The subsidy, if one is funded, is bounded, expiring and netted out of the same inflow — it is not a second funding source. TAIKO has no mint path today, so paying rewards in new TAIKO would require a token upgrade and is not a standing source. No rule may be conditioned on TAIKO’s price rising, and there is no verified price, float or liquidity data in this design.

Long-range attacks are handled by weak subjectivity

An attacker who once held a third of the stake and has since exited can still produce an alternative history whose signatures verify, because it kept the keys that were committed at that past epoch; once its stake is released it cannot be punished. The defences are that a node never adopts an L2 history conflicting with its L1 checkpoint, and that it refuses to bootstrap from a checkpoint older than a fixed freshness window. That window is the weak-subjectivity trust requirement: a node cannot validate its way out of it, and if no fresh, Ethereum-final checkpoint exists — because of a long halt or a proving outage — a new node must not present the history it syncs as verified, and new validators cannot be admitted until a newer checkpoint exists.

Governance can change the rules

Upgrades are a governance action, and a governance action can rewrite any rule in this design, including the ones this page describes. Malicious or captured governance is outside the protocol’s protection: the staking ledger, the offence catalogue and the reward identity are all rules that an upgrade could replace. The design's protection in ordinary operation is the absence of an emergency operator or an owner-triggered path that moves bonded stake — not an assumption that governance will always behave. Liveness after a stall is the one place where the design depends on governance doing something: that dependency is stated as a trust assumption above, not hidden inside this section.

No measured numbers, and no compensation

Proving latency, throughput, bandwidth, storage, retention, operator cost, fee revenue, reward schedules, L1 verification gas, evidence windows and the withdrawal delay are all unmeasured. Penalties are not compensation and no universal bound on user loss exists: a penalty debits an offender’s stake, and losses from a broken safety assumption, a key compromise, a proof-system failure, a malicious upgrade or an L1 reorganisation are not covered by any rule.

What this gives you

  • A clear boundary: which claims are arguments, which are arithmetic, and which numbers simply do not exist yet.
  • An explicit statement that a settled checkpoint is never rewritten while everything above it is provisional and can be discarded by a timelocked, resume-only restart — and that where no restart can help, a halt can still be permanent.
  • A restart that is narrow by construction: it resumes from the last accepted checkpoint, chooses no state, checkpoint or height, cannot be shortened once queued, is void if the chain makes progress first, and advances the recovery generation so certificates from the discarded range are void.
  • A precise statement of the inclusion limit: a narrow, proof-enforced obligation over data published to L1 (an upper bound on exclusion), no general inclusion list, and for unpublished data only statistical resistance, with no remedy against isolation.
  • The dependencies named — retention, funding, weak subjectivity, governance, and governance liveness after a stall — so they can be monitored and priced deliberately.

What it does not give you

  • No implementation, deployment, audit, benchmark or model to point at.
  • No measurement of any performance, cost or economic quantity of this design.
  • No rewrite of settled history, no emergency operator and no degraded mode — but also no compensation for the off-chain losses a restart can cause, and no universal bound on them; and no protocol-level guarantee that a stalled chain resumes at all.
  • No per-transaction inclusion guarantee, no general inclusion list, no retention enforcement and no compensation for loss.
  • No protection against a malicious upgrade, or against a proof-system or cryptographic failure.

Check yourself

Settlement stalls for a week. What does the design do about it?

After the stall threshold the chain halts safely: nothing at or below the last checkpoint accepted on Ethereum is rewritten, and a withdrawal whose signal is at or below that checkpoint stays executable on Ethereum alone. Restarting takes a governance decision: a timelocked, resume-only restart that resumes from the last accepted checkpoint and discards everything above it, void if a batch lands first. A transaction in the discarded range is lost and must be resubmitted. If the missing ingredient is data at or below the accepted checkpoint, no restart can help and the halt can be permanent. And if the real cause is an empty or below-cost reward pool, a restart does not fix it: it resumes from a checkpoint and discards a range, but it pays no reward and changes no fee. Whether the chain resumes at all depends on governance acting — there is no protocol-level liveness guarantee.

What happens if governance never acts after a stall?

The chain stays halted. The design has no automatic or permissionless restart, and no protocol-level guarantee of liveness after a stall. Users can still exit from the last settled state on Ethereum alone (MEM-15), but unsettled value above the checkpoint has no L1-provable claim until a later checkpoint covers it. This is the disclosed social-layer trust assumption, not a temporary gap.

A user is isolated from every honest proposer. Can they withdraw anyway?

Only if the transaction’s data reaches L1. Publishing is permissionless, and once the published record is due a batch must resolve it within the capped prefix, so exclusion is time-bounded and the withdrawal can start. But the narrow rule begins only at publication: an adversary that can also keep the data off L1 — by cutting the user off from every honest proposer, or by censoring L1 inclusion of the publication itself — can still prevent the withdrawal from starting, indefinitely, and the protocol offers no remedy. For data that never reaches L1, inclusion resistance is a conditional statistical property, not a guarantee.

Which numbers in this course are measured?

None of this design’s own numbers. Ethereum constants such as the blob gas cost and blob sizes are copied from published standards, and quantities like 900 blocks per thirty minutes are arithmetic on the two-second cadence. Everything else — proving latency, throughput, operator cost, fee revenue, reward rates, evidence and withdrawal delays — is unmeasured.

Assumptions

  • Fewer than one third of the voting power in each epoch is malicious, and enough validators stay online to reach quorum.
  • Somebody retains each batch’s data and witness until its proof lands.
  • Somebody publishes a transaction’s data to L1 while the blobs are retrievable, and L1 includes that publication; if the data never reaches L1, the narrow obligation never begins.
  • Ethereum keeps producing and finalizing blocks, and L1 facts become Ethereum-final at a known depth.
  • The reward pool is funded by L2 fee revenue or a recorded top-up; otherwise no reward is payable.
  • A fresh, Ethereum-final L1 checkpoint is available to a new node, and governance does not execute an upgrade that breaks these rules.
  • The stall trigger T_STALL_GOV and the timelock T_GOV_RESUME are unmeasured, and the timelock is long enough that every user can exit from the last settled state before a restart executes.
  • Governance acts to restart a stalled chain. The design has no protocol-level guarantee of liveness after a stall; this is a social-layer trust assumption, and it is the dependency the limitations above state plainly.
