What Etna PoS + ZK is

This lesson gives you the shape of the system: who does what, what Ethereum still provides, and the five questions people mix up when they talk about it.

This is a design, not a running system. Nothing described here is implemented, deployed, audited or measured. Numbers are tagged derived (arithmetic on the design's fixed inputs), sourced (an external standard or constant) or unmeasured (a placeholder with no measurement behind it). The authoritative rules are in the specification.

The idea

Etna PoS + ZK is a layer-2 chain that keeps its own consensus. Its blocks are ordered by validators who bond the TAIKO token on Ethereum and whose voting power is the stake they bonded. Ethereum does not choose the order of L2 transactions, and no Ethereum proposer sits in the L2 block path. The honest name for the architecture is a PoS-sequenced validity rollup (GEN-09).

Blocks are produced on a fixed cadence: one L2 block every two seconds under the design's operating assumptions derived, not measured. A block becomes final when more than two thirds of the epoch's voting power has signed a precommit for it (CONS-05). Finality is a consensus fact, established by validators alone in a few message round trips — it is not an Ethereum confirmation and it is not a proof.

Ethereum's job is different. Batches of L2 blocks are published to Ethereum together with a zero-knowledge proof that the batch's blocks were finalized by the validator set and executed correctly. One Ethereum transaction carries the batch data and the proof, or the batch is not accepted at all (L1-01). There is no state on that path between "transaction exists" and "batch accepted": the contract derives what the proof is checked against, the proof verifies, and only then is a checkpoint written. A batch whose data is missing cannot be accepted, and a batch is never recorded before its proof verifies.

Data and proof travel together because the alternative has a known failure mode: data published first, proof never produced, and then the data expires from Ethereum's blob window while the batch is still unsettled. Here the canonical copy of a batch's data is the accepting L1 transaction — its calldata or its blob sidecars — so acceptance and publication are the same event (DA-01).

Finally, safety beats progress. When the information or participation needed to continue safely is missing, the protocol stops producing and keeps serving what it has (HALT-01). It never replaces a block it already finalized, and there is no operator key, pause button or emergency path that can rewrite finalized history.

What Ethereum provides, and what it does not

Still provided by Ethereum

  • Data availability. The canonical copy of a batch's data is the accepting L1 transaction itself: its calldata and/or its blob sidecars. A batch is never accepted without it.
  • Proof verification. An L1 contract checks the proof and writes the checkpoint only if verification succeeds, in the same transaction.
  • Settlement. A monotone checkpoint chain records, per landed height, the L2 block hash, state root, epoch, validator-set root, data commitment and the L1 block that accepted it.
  • Bridge anchoring. Cross-chain messages authenticate against a checkpoint that the Inbox wrote; value leaves only on the strength of an L1-accepted checkpoint plus the required delay.

No longer provided by Ethereum

  • Ordering. The order of L2 transactions is decided by L2 PoS. No Ethereum proposer, builder or slot timing enters a validity rule.
  • Per-transaction inclusion. There is no inclusion duty and no L1 forced-inclusion path. Resistance to censorship is a conditional statistical property of proposer rotation, not a guarantee.
  • Instant settlement. L2 finality (a few round trips) is a different event from L1 acceptance (proof + inclusion) and from Ethereum finality (a further L1 finality lag).
  • Rollback of finalized L2 history. No rule, timeout, rotation, admission rule or ordinary upgrade may invalidate a PoS-finalized block. If the chain cannot continue correctly, it halts.

What this gives you

  • A clear split of responsibilities: validators order and finalize; provers and Ethereum settle; the bridge authenticates against settled checkpoints.
  • Permissionless roles with objective entry conditions — bond TAIKO to validate, produce a proof to land a batch — and no allowlists in ordinary operation.
  • An architecture whose failure mode for missing information is a halt, not a silent rewrite of finalized history.
  • A vocabulary — the eight statuses used across this course — that lets a tool say exactly which guarantee it is showing.

What it does not give you

  • No implementation. There is no compiled client, contract deployment, audit, benchmark or testnet result behind any of this.
  • No measured performance. The 2 s cadence, the 30-minute proving envelope, the round budget and the fleet sizing are all unmeasured; the arithmetic in this lesson is arithmetic, not evidence.
  • No safety beyond the stake assumption. If one third or more of an epoch's voting power is malicious or compromised, two conflicting certificates can both be valid, and the proof verifies both.
  • No inclusion guarantee. Proposer selection is public and predictable; the protocol has no per-transaction inclusion promise and no L1 escape hatch.
  • No fully trustless L2 checkpoint write yet. The design retains one reserved system sender for the L2-side checkpoint write; its only permitted action is to publish the Ethereum-final L1 fact that the rule already constrains.
  • No unconditional blob-path binding. Batch data can travel as calldata or as blobs. The calldata commitment is the unconditional path; the blob path's fixed-point argument must be independently checked and its in-guest evaluation cost measured before it is relied on.

Check yourself

A tool tells you a transaction is "confirmed". What should you ask?

Which of the eight statuses it means. A 2 s PoS confirmation says a validator set agreed on ordering; it does not say the batch data and proof are on Ethereum. Until the accepting L1 transaction exists and is final, a settlement or bridge action cannot safely rely on it.

Why can a batch not be published to Ethereum before its proof?

Because the canonical copy of the data is the accepting transaction. If data could be published first, it could sit on Ethereum unproven, age out of the blob window, and leave a settled-looking obligation that no one can prove — which would force a rollback the design forbids. So data and proof are one atomic acceptance or nothing.

Ethereum verifies the proof. Does that mean the L2 history is safe even if one third of validators misbehave?

No. The proof attests that the presented certificate is internally consistent with the authenticated validator set and the quorum rule. It cannot attest that no competing certificate exists. If the stake assumption fails, two conflicting certificates can both be cryptographically valid and both can produce accepting proofs.

Assumptions

  • Fewer than one third of each epoch's voting power is malicious or compromised.
  • More than two thirds of each epoch's voting power is online and messages arrive within the round-timeout bound often enough to complete rounds.
  • Validators and full nodes hold and serve the data of blocks they voted for, at least until settlement; somebody keeps the data until the proof lands.
  • At least one adequately-resourced prover completes each committed batch.
  • Ethereum includes valid transactions within a bounded delay and does not reorganise below the finality depth it reports.
  • TAIKO has a market, and the stake bonded is worth enough that slashing matters.
  • The DAO executes upgrades honestly and never uses an upgrade to invalidate finalized history.
