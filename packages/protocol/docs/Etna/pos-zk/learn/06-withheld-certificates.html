06 — The certificate nobody can see

Problem this lesson solves. A leader can distribute a block to only some participants, keep the finality certificate private, let everyone else time out and change leader, and then reveal the certificate — this lesson explains why that schedule cannot make two conflicting histories final, and why an elapsed timeout is never proof that something does not exist.

In plain language

Selective distribution. A block reaches a validator in two pieces: the block body (transactions and state) and the consensus messages about it. A leader can send the body to a subset and to nobody else. Message delivery inside a bound is an assumption, not an enforcement (A-CONS-3, T-1), so nothing forces a leader to talk to everyone. What a validator will not do is vote for a block it has not received and validated; the specification's withholding catalogue records that consequence directly — a withheld body has no safety effect, it only stops the height from advancing (WH-01).

The harder object to hide is the certificate. "PoS-finalized" is not a feeling that a block is winning. It is a status with fixed evidence: a published commit certificate over (chain id, epoch, height, block hash) carrying strictly more than two thirds of the epoch's voting power, plus the receiver's own data-availability check for the block body (STATUS-04). The reveal-later attack asks: if a certificate exists but almost nobody has seen it, can everyone else legitimately finalize a conflicting block at that height, and then be contradicted when the old certificate appears?

No — the certificate's visibility was never what protected safety; the lock is. A validator that precommitted block B at round R is locked on B. It will not prevote a conflicting block at the same height unless it sees a proof-of-lock-change (PoLC) for a different value at a strictly later round at that same height — and it must verify that PoLC itself, not trust a hint field in a proposal (CONS-04). A commit certificate means more than two thirds of the voting power precommitted B. With fewer than one third of that power Byzantine, more than one third is honest and therefore locked. A conflicting block cannot assemble the more than two thirds it needs, because those locked validators will not vote for it. This is the published Proof of Safety, quoted by the specification: a certificate that exists but has not been published can delay a node that has not seen it, but cannot displace a decision already taken (CONS-04).

A view change changes the leader, not the locks. Round timeouts are durations in seconds with an adaptive ladder, and a stalled round advances locally (CONS-07). Advancing does not unlock a validator, and CONS-07(5) says plainly that a timeout is never evidence that a certificate, proposal or block does not exist. STATUS-10 states the same prohibition as a general rule for the whole design: no rule may conclude that a certificate, proof or block does not exist because a timeout elapsed; timeouts trigger local actions only.

The lock survives reconfiguration. An epoch boundary carries the previous epoch's last finalized block forward as an anchor and carries locks across it (CONS-09), and certificates are judged under the set of their own epoch and remain valid forever (CONS-08). So a stakeholder that rotates keys, changes stake or joins a later epoch cannot escape a lock it established earlier.

The reveal-later schedule is written down as a mandatory test in WH-04: selective distribution, local voting, private certificate, alternative path (leader change, validator change, recovery), then revelation. The required conclusion is that two conflicting histories cannot both receive the mode's irreversible guarantee, and it rests on three mechanisms together — locks, view changes, and reconfiguration — never on signature counting alone.

Precise definitions

  • Commit certificate. The specification defines it as the evidence that more than two thirds of an epoch's voting power precommitted a block at a (height, round, block id); its publication plus a receiver's own data check is what yields STATUS-04 (CONS-05, glossary).
  • Lock and proof-of-lock-change (PoLC). The lock is a persistent local pair (locked value, locked round) that survives restart and is never cleared at a height or an epoch boundary. Unlocking happens only against a PoLC for a different value at a strictly later round at the same height; the proposal's pol_round field is advisory and must be verified, not trusted (CONS-04).
  • View change. A local advance to the next round after the round's timeout expires or a PoLC for the current round has been processed; the leader rotates by rule, and a timeout changes only local round state (CONS-07).
  • Equivocation. Two signed messages of the same type (proposal, prevote or precommit) at the same height and round with different signed values. Signing a prevote for X and a precommit for a different Y at the same round is not equivocation — it is the normal step sequence. The evidence needs only the two signed objects and the L1-committed set root (CONS-11).
  • Withholding catalogue. WH-01 lists each withheld object (block body, individual votes, aggregate signatures, finality certificate, validator-transition certificate, execution witness, completed proof), its safety effect, its liveness effect, the honest action, and whether objective evidence exists. In no case does non-receipt justify concluding that an object does not exist, and in no case does non-receipt alone justify slashing.
  • Objective evidence versus local suspicion. WH-02 fixes the boundary: only self-contained, on-chain-verifiable evidence may trigger a penalty or a recovery action. A message that did not arrive, a height that did not advance, or a proof that has not been submitted is local suspicion, and may trigger only local actions such as retry, view change or disclosure.
  • Reveal-later certificate analysis. WH-04 is the normative analysis of the schedule above; its premises are A-CONS-1 in every epoch, correct validators enforcing CONS-02 and CONS-04, and the epoch-handoff argument of CONS-09, which the specification marks assumed-with-argument and names as review target F1.
  • Safety invariant. Uniqueness of the finalized prefix, with every premise named, including that the Byzantine stake is strictly below one third in every epoch (CONS-12).

Read the table with its premises attached: fewer than one third of the epoch's power is Byzantine in that epoch; correct validators implement vote uniqueness and the lock rule (CONS-02, CONS-04); and the epoch handoff carries locks across the boundary (CONS-09, assumed-with-argument, review target F1). The last two rows are the two independent answers to the reveal-later schedule: the lock arithmetic above, and, if a conflicting certificate somehow existed anyway, the fork-accountability intersection that makes more than one third of the power slashable. The specification does not claim that a hidden certificate is harmless in general; it claims it is harmless to safety, while a withheld certificate can stall the nodes that have not seen it — a liveness cost, disclosed as such.

Attacks this prevents

  • Reveal-later invalidation. A certificate revealed after an alternative path progressed cannot be used to invalidate a decision taken after a timeout, because the alternative path could not have committed a conflicting block at that height in the first place (WH-04).
  • "Wait and see nothing" finality. A rule that commits only if no conflicting certificate arrives would make a local decision depend on an unprovable negative; CONS-05 forbids conditioning a commitment on not having seen another certificate (CONS-05).
  • Timeout-as-absence. Treating an expired round timer as evidence that a certificate or block does not exist — the failure mode STATUS-10 names, and the mechanism by which a withheld certificate would otherwise appear to undo the past (STATUS-10).
  • False accusations. Slashing a validator because its message did not arrive; only self-contained evidence such as two conflicting signed messages may trigger a penalty (WH-02, CONS-11).
  • Lock erasure. Losing a lock on restart, on a view change, or at an epoch boundary, which would let a validator vote for a conflicting block without any slashable equivocation (CONS-04, HALT-02).

Attacks this does not prevent

  • A stalled height. If a leader withholds the body from more than a third of the power, the height cannot advance. That is a liveness effect, and the honest outcome is a stall or a safe halt, not a rewritten history (WH-01, HALT-01).
  • A permanently withheld body or certificate. The specification discloses that a permanently withheld PoLC stalls a locked validator by design (CONS-04).
  • One third or more Byzantine, compromised keys, or a client that mis-implements the lock rules. Then the premise fails, the argument fails with it, and two conflicting certificates can both be cryptographically valid (CONS-12, A-CONS-1).
  • Proving, data availability, or L1 problems. A withheld completed proof is a different catalogue entry, handled by permissionless re-proving and the settlement backpressure cap (WH-01, HALT-03).
  • Detection rates. For every detection rule the specification states both error directions and records the rates as unmeasured; no rule may claim a detection rate (WH-03).

Self-check

Why is an unpublished certificate harmless to safety?

Because safety rests on the lock, not on publication. A validator that precommitted B is locked and will not prevote a conflicting value without a strictly later-round PoLC at the same height (CONS-04). Hiding the certificate delays the nodes that have not seen it; it does not release the locks that block a competing commit.

What exactly must a locked validator see before it may unlock?

A PoLC for a value different from its locked value, at a strictly later round at the same height — and the validator must verify that PoLC itself. A PoLC at the same round, at an earlier round, at another height, or for the value already locked does not unlock; the pol_round field of a proposal is a hint, not evidence (CONS-04).

A node waits the full round budget, sees no certificate, and changes leader. What has it learned?

Nothing about whether a certificate exists elsewhere. It has performed a local action permitted by the timeout ladder (CONS-07); any conclusion of the form "no certificate exists" is forbidden by STATUS-10. If it is locked, it keeps its lock and will accept the certificate whenever it arrives.

Challenge this. The lesson used these assumptions, all of them named in the specification: (i) A-CONS-1 — fewer than one third of the epoch's voting power is Byzantine in that epoch; (ii) correct validators enforce vote uniqueness and the lock rule (CONS-02, CONS-04), including keeping locks across restart (HALT-02 — an implementation gate); (iii) the epoch handoff carries locks across a boundary, which CONS-09 marks assumed-with-argument (review target F1); (iv) the worked example's stake unit and its mapping to integer voting power are illustrative and unmeasured (MEM-02); (v) "the certificate is valid forever" is a rule about judgement, not about anyone still holding the bytes (CONS-08). Falsifier: find a step in the reveal-later schedule where the alternative path discards a lock, or where a validator-set change resets locks — that would be a defect under WH-04.
