When things go wrong

This lesson gives you the failure-handling rules: what happens when a proposer hides a block, when votes or certificates do not arrive, when a set goes offline, when data cannot be found or when settlement stalls — and the guarantee class that follows: a confirmation above the last batch accepted on Ethereum is provisional, and a bonded, delayed, permissionless recovery can replace it. This is a design description; none of it is implemented, measured, audited or deployed.

The idea

Failures are ordinary inputs to this protocol, not exceptions bolted on afterwards. Two rules do most of the work.

A timeout is never evidence of absence. When a timer expires, a node may change what it does — retry, change the view, halt — but it may not conclude that a vote, a certificate or a block does not exist somewhere else, and it may not invalidate anything (STATUS-10). A certificate that is revealed after everyone gave up is still the valid certificate.

A validator’s lock keeps two histories from both being certified. A validator that precommits a block at a height is locked on it. It will not prevote a conflicting block at that height unless it sees a proof-of-lock-change at the same height and a strictly later round (CONS-04). A certified height implies more than a third of the voting power is honest and locked, so a conflicting certificate would need those validators to break the lock — which is exactly the behaviour the rule forbids and slashing punishes.

A confirmation above the last batch accepted on Ethereum is provisional. The L2 certificate is real, and more than two thirds of the epoch’s voting power signed it; but until the batch carrying it is accepted on L1 with its proof, the protocol’s own rules can replace it. That is the guarantee class of every confirmation above the checkpoint, and every interface that shows one must say so (STATUS-04, STATUS-11).

A settlement stall is the one thing that triggers a recovery. A stall means that no batch extending the current L1 checkpoint has been accepted for T_STALL — long enough that a normal slow proof, or a single prover failure inside the normal envelope, cannot be the explanation. The condition is measured on L1, so no participant’s local view can declare it early (REC-02). Anyone may then start a recovery, permissionlessly, by posting a bond on L1. There is no operator, no DAO vote and no validator quorum in the path.

Every attempt costs the invoker money, and a successful rollback is not free either. The invocation splits the bond at once: a non-refundable component goes to the protocol treasury and is never returned, and only the remainder — the refundable balance — is returned if the recovery completes. The invoker pays the non-refundable component on every attempt, whether the recovery completes, is cancelled, or fails in any other way, and a cooldown follows every attempt. The bond escalates with the number of attempts in a rolling window, counting attempts and never completions. That is deliberate: a rollback that succeeds destroys provisional history, so it must not be free, and a rollback that fails must cost something too, so recovery cannot be spammed against honest progress.

Honest progress cancels it, and cancellation runs to Ethereum finality. The recovery takes effect only when its completion transaction is Ethereum-final. If any valid batch extending the checkpoint recorded at invocation is accepted after the invocation and before the completion transaction is included, the recovery is cancelled and the whole bond — non-refundable component and refundable balance together — goes to the protocol treasury, in the same transaction that accepts the batch; the completion transition reverts once that predicate holds. A certificate or a proof for a discarded height does not cancel it, because those heights are by construction not yet settled. Because cancellation and effect are both resolved at Ethereum finality, there is no moment at which a completed recovery and a batch at a discarded height are both true.

When it completes, the discarded heights are permanently retired. Everything above the last checkpoint accepted on Ethereum — inclusions, ordering, balances and bridge messages that were only provisional — is discarded, and the chain resumes from that checkpoint. Checkpoints and batches already accepted on Ethereum are never touched, and neither is anything below them. The completion transition records resumeHeight, the last discarded height plus one, and every height below it is retired permanently: it can never be certified or landed again, by anyone, at any generation. That is what stops a discarded branch from being re-proven and reinstated. The recovery generation is a public input of the proof statement, but it only invalidates proofs that were already generated; the property that makes the discarded range unlandable is the retired height, because a batch is judged by where it starts and must start at or above resumeHeight (L1-06, PRF-02, REC-02). A rolled-back L2 transaction is not replayed automatically: it must be resubmitted, and inclusion is not guaranteed. Off-chain losses are not compensated by any protocol penalty (ECON-11).

Locks at retired heights are void. A validator that was locked on a block in the discarded range is not stuck: a lock at a retired height carries no authority, it is not evidence that the validator misbehaved, and it is not a reason to halt. Without that carve-out, a locked third of the power could wedge the new chain at a height that no longer exists. A late certificate for a discarded height is likewise void for canonical purposes, though it remains admissible as evidence of what its signers signed (CONS-04, CONS-12).

Where recovery cannot help, the chain still halts. Halting remains the correct action for missing data at or below the accepted checkpoint, for a missing validator-set commitment, for an L1 view that disagrees with consensus, and for a failure of the stake assumption. In those cases no rule-legal action can restore progress, and a halt can be permanent (HALT-01).

How it works

Withholding: what can be hidden, and what it costs

The design assumes messages can be late, lost or delivered selectively. For each object that can be withheld, the honest action is fixed (WH-01). No case of non-receipt justifies concluding that an object does not exist, and no case of non-receipt alone justifies slashing.

What is evidence, and what is only suspicion

A protocol penalty needs objective, self-contained, on-chain-verifiable evidence: two signed messages of the same type at the same height and round with different block ids, or a signed message that violates a rule whose inputs are all on-chain (WH-02). Everything else — a message that did not arrive, a height that did not advance, a proof that has not been submitted, a peer that did not answer — is local suspicion. It can change local behaviour: retry, view change, peer selection, disclosure. It cannot trigger a penalty or a statement about what exists elsewhere. Silence is not provable.

The lock, stated precisely

A validator’s lock is a local triple (locked_height, locked_value, locked_round). It is per height: it constrains votes at that height only, it survives a restart while the height is unresolved, and it is released in exactly three ways:

  • the locked value commits at that height within the current generation — once the value the validator locked on commits, the lock has done its work; or
  • the validator sees a proof-of-lock-change at the same height H, for a different value, at a round strictly later than its locked_round and earlier than the current round; or
  • the height is retired by a completed recovery — every lock at a retired height is void.

Nothing else unlocks. A proof-of-lock-change at the same round, at an earlier round, or for the value already locked does not unlock; a proof-of-lock-change at another height neither unlocks this height nor creates a lock there. The pol_round field carried in a proposal is only a hint; the validator must verify the proof itself. And a validator that holds a lock at a height and then sees a valid commit certificate for a different block at that height has evidence that the assumptions failed: it halts, and it does not treat the certificate as an unlock. The retired-height case is the exception: a certificate for a discarded height is void for canonical purposes, the lock is void with it, and the validator is neither stuck nor required to halt.

Why this protects safety: a commit certificate requires more than two thirds of the epoch’s voting power. If a second, conflicting commit certificate existed, the two signer sets would overlap in more than one third of the power, and those validators would have had to sign conflicting blocks at the same height. The lock rule says the honest ones never do. A certificate that exists but has not been published can delay a node that has not seen it; it cannot displace a decision.

Halt: what it is, and what it costs

A correct validator halts — stops signing and stops advancing its certified view — when any of the following holds and no rule-legal action can resolve it (HALT-01):

  • it cannot obtain the data for a certified or locked block;
  • it cannot obtain a quorum for a height within the timeout ladder;
  • the L1 facts it must consume are not yet Ethereum-final, or its L1 view disagrees with the consensus view;
  • the L1 validator-set commitment for the epoch it is about to enter is absent; or
  • the unsettled-depth cap would be exceeded.

Halting is not a fork, not a rollback and not a vote to abandon the chain. A halting node keeps serving what it has and keeps its state so it can resume. There is no emergency key, no operator override and no governance rescue that can advance, rewind or replace the checkpoint (HALT-04). If progress needs information that no rule-legal action can obtain, the protocol says so and stops.

A pure settlement stall is different: it has a rule-legal action, the recovery. Halting remains the correct outcome when the missing ingredient is data or participation that recovery cannot replace — anything at or below the last checkpoint accepted on Ethereum.

Recovery, step by step

  1. Trigger. Only one condition starts it: a settlement stall — no batch extending the current L1 checkpoint has been accepted for T_STALL. The threshold is derived from the permitted proof latency plus the settlement pipeline, L1 finality and a margin, and it is strictly larger than the proof envelope, so a normal slow proof cannot trigger it. It is measured on L1, and no participant’s local view can declare it early.
  2. Invocation. Anyone posts the bond in an L1 transaction containing only the claim, the checkpoint it is made against and the bond. At invocation the bond splits: a non-refundable component goes to the protocol treasury at once and is never returned, and the rest is a refundable balance. The bond escalates as B_REC(e) = B_REC_BASE · 2attempts, where attempts counts invocations in a rolling window — completed, cancelled or failed alike — and a cooldown follows every attempt. No DAO, operator or validator quorum is involved, and the invoker chooses nothing: not the restored checkpoint, not the validator set, not any configuration value.
  3. Delay and cancellation. The completion transition may be called by any account no earlier than T_RECOVERY_DELAY after the invocation, while the same bond remains posted. Acceptance of any valid batch extending the checkpoint recorded at invocation during that window cancels the recovery and sends the whole bond to the protocol treasury, and the completion transition reverts once that predicate holds. The reveal of a certificate or proof for a discarded height does not cancel it, because those heights are not yet settled.
  4. Completion, at Ethereum finality. The recovery takes effect only when its completion transaction is Ethereum-final; L2 nodes do not act on the completion record before that depth. The transition writes resumeHeight — the last discarded height plus one — and increments the recovery generation by exactly one. Because cancellation and effect are both resolved at finality, there is no instant at which a completed recovery and a batch at a discarded height are both true. If the completion transaction is reorganised out below finality it never took effect: the pre-recovery history stands and the refundable balance stays posted.
  5. Retired heights. Every discarded height is permanently retired: it can never be certified or landed again, by anyone, at any generation. A batch must start at max(lastLandedHeight + 1, resumeHeight), so a start below resumeHeight is rejected while a start exactly at resumeHeight is the correct, landable case. Correct validators must not sign a proposal below it, and a retired height can never be re-certified. The generation counter stays as a cheap invalidation of proofs that were already generated; the property that a discarded branch cannot come back rests on the retired heights, because the acceptance rule is about where a batch starts, not which proof was used.
  6. Locks at retired heights. On completion, every lock at a retired height is void. A validator that was locked on a discarded block is not stuck, and the old locked value is not evidence of misbehaviour and not a reason to halt. A late certificate for a discarded height is void for canonical purposes but remains admissible as evidence of what was signed.
  7. Afterwards. A late proof for a discarded height is void because the height is retired; its data commitment may be re-batched by any proposer, but only as part of a new batch that starts at or above resumeHeight and is proved against the restored checkpoint. A rolled-back transaction is not replayed and must be resubmitted. A new recovery can be invoked only after the cooldown that follows every attempt has elapsed, and each attempt costs more because the bond carries a non-refundable component that is never returned and grows with the number of attempts in the window. A repeat after a recovery has already completed is permitted but harmless when nothing above the checkpoint has been certified since: it retires nothing new, cannot lower the retirement floor, and leaves the checkpoint and its state unchanged, so the invoker gains nothing and pays the non-refundable component.

What a halt, and what a recovery, mean for users

  • Below the accepted checkpoint, funds are not lost; exit is delayed. A withdrawal needs the settlement pipeline to resume. A halt delays withdrawals while nothing is forfeited (MSG-03).
  • Above the accepted checkpoint, a confirmation can be replaced, and the discarded range is retired. A recovery discards that range and resumes from the last checkpoint accepted on Ethereum, and the discarded heights can never be certified or landed again, at any generation. A transaction in the replaced range is not replayed automatically and must be resubmitted.
  • Accepted history stays valid. Anything already anchored to a checkpoint or batch accepted on L1 remains authenticated, and recovery may not touch it.
  • Off-chain losses are not compensated. A trade, a signed message or a position built on a provisional confirmation has no protocol remedy (ECON-11).
  • Validators are not punished for halting. Halting is the required behaviour when the rules say so.
  • Operators must not improvise. “Unsticking” the chain by publishing data without a proof, or picking a branch, converts a safe halt into an unsafe restart. The only legitimate actions are the rule-legal ones, and for a settlement stall the rule-legal action is the published recovery.
  • A halt below the checkpoint may still be permanent. If every holder of an accepted block’s data has disappeared, nothing in the protocol can reconstruct it, and recovery may not replace accepted history. That is a disclosed liveness cost of permanent settlement.

Restarting afterwards

After a halt, a correct participant resumes only from the highest certified block whose data and certificate it can independently reconstruct (HALT-02). It never adopts an alternative history at a height it has already certified, and it never treats a timeout during the halt as evidence that a certificate does not exist. On restart four rules apply:

  1. Each validator enforces a signed-message uniqueness check over its own signing history: no message of the same type at the same height and round may be signed twice, including after a restart or a resync. A validator that cannot prove it has not already signed for a (height, round, type) must not sign — lost memory is treated as a signing fault, not as a fresh start.
  2. A resuming validator re-verifies the epoch and the validator set for the height it is resuming at instead of trusting its cache.
  3. Replayed historical messages are rejected: every vote carries its epoch and round, and receiving rules reject messages for heights already certified.
  4. A resumed node’s first act is a data-and-certificate sync, not a proposal.

Recovery is not a restart of settled history: it restores the last checkpoint accepted on Ethereum and resumes from there, and HALT-02’s rules still govern what a node may do at heights it has already certified. A node must not treat a discarded height as certified, and a lock at a retired height neither binds it nor obliges it to halt.

Why unsettled history is replaced instead of halting forever

An unbounded halt is not free: if settlement stops and nothing in the protocol can restart it, the chain stops serving users, and the only remedies are outside the protocol. The design keeps the settled part settled and makes the unsettled part explicitly provisional. A checkpoint or batch accepted on Ethereum is never rewritten; history above it can be replaced by a bonded, delayed, permissionless recovery whose invoker pays on every attempt, whatever the outcome, and whose discarded heights are then retired so they cannot return. That is the trade: an outage-free path back to a working chain, paid for with a weaker guarantee above the last accepted checkpoint and with a permanent cut at the resume height.

The resistance argument is not a claim that abuse is impossible. Recovery is a manipulation primitive with a price: an adversary who can cause or prolong a stall may be able to replace unfavourable unsettled history. The non-refundable bond component paid on every attempt, the delay, cancellation by any valid batch, the treasury destination of the whole bond on cancellation, the escalation that counts attempts and the cooldown after each attempt are the defences, and the conditions under which the argument would fail are stated with the rule (REC-03). The argument also depends on the reward pool actually being funded, which is an economic premise and not a rule.

A lock, walked through. A validator is locked on block B at (H, round 2). At round 5 it receives a proposal for a different block B′ whose hint says pol_round = 4. The hint is not evidence: the validator must obtain and verify the proof-of-lock-change itself. If that proof is for B′ at (H, round 4), it unlocks and prevotes B′, because 4 is strictly later than its locked round 2 and the value differs. If the proof is for round 2, or for B, it stays locked and prevotes B. If instead it obtains a commit certificate for a different block at H, it halts — it does not treat the certificate as an unlock. If the height has since been retired by a completed recovery, however, the lock is void: the validator does not halt on the old value and takes part in the new history from resumeHeight onward.

A prover that never finishes, and then a recovery. A batch is certified at time t. At t + 30 minutes there is still no proof. Nothing special happens yet: a proof consuming the whole envelope is normal, not misconduct; other provers may produce a proof for the same data commitment and land it, and the first valid landing wins. If in addition no batch extending the current checkpoint is accepted for T_STALL, the settlement-stall trigger is met. Anyone may then post a bond and start a recovery, paying the non-refundable component at invocation and accepting a cooldown before another attempt. During T_RECOVERY_DELAY any valid batch cancels it and the whole bond goes to the protocol treasury. If nothing lands, the completion transition may be called; it takes effect when it is Ethereum-final. The chain then resumes from the last checkpoint accepted on Ethereum, and every block above it — including the certified batch and any transaction in it — is replaced and retired: those heights can never be certified or landed again, at any generation, and the discarded range cannot be re-proven back into canonical history. A validator locked at a retired height is not stuck. A rolled-back transaction must be resubmitted; nothing replays it.

What this gives you

  • A fixed, minimal response for every class of missing message or data, without inventing rules at run time.
  • Safety that does not depend on seeing a certificate: the lock protects the decision, not the publication.
  • A principled restart: resume from the highest certified block whose data and certificate can be reconstructed, never from a competing branch.
  • A settled prefix that is never rewritten and a rule-legal path for everything above it, so a settlement stall is not a permanent outage.
  • A priced, permissionless recovery: no operator and no DAO; every attempt pays a non-refundable component whether or not it completes, cancellation sends the whole bond to the protocol treasury, and escalation counts attempts.
  • Permanent retirement of discarded heights: a rolled-back range can never be certified or landed again, so a discarded branch cannot be re-proven and reinstated.
  • Void locks at retired heights, so a validator locked on a discarded block is not wedged and the old locked value is not treated as misbehaviour.

What it does not give you

  • Availability. If data for a block at or below the accepted checkpoint is gone, the halt can be permanent; retention is an assumption, not an enforced duty.
  • Permanent confirmations above the last accepted checkpoint. A certified block can be replaced by a recovery, a rolled-back transaction must be resubmitted, and off-chain losses are not compensated.
  • A free successful rollback. Completion returns only the refundable balance; the non-refundable component is already gone, and every failed attempt costs one too.
  • A selective rollback, or a route back for discarded history. Recovery restores the checkpoint whole and rewinds the contiguous range above it wholesale: the invoker has no choice about which transactions inside that range survive, so recovery cannot be used to pick winners. That is a property of the rewind, not a promise about the outcome — unwound transactions are not replayed automatically and their inclusion is not guaranteed, so a party whose transaction was unwound must resubmit it. Heights below resumeHeight can never be certified or landed again, at any generation.
  • A recovery that cannot itself be abused. It is a manipulation primitive with a price, and the argument that a sub-threshold coalition cannot force the trigger is conditional, not proven.
  • Punishment for silence. Only conflicting signed messages are objectively provable; a node that never answers is not slashable.
  • A degraded or read-only mode. A minority outage halts the chain rather than continuing with reduced participation.
  • An operator unstick button. A settlement stall can be cleared only by the permissionless, bonded, delayed recovery — not by an operator, DAO or emergency key.
  • Any measured detection rate, timeout value, recovery parameter or retention window. Those are not yet measured.

Check yourself

Validators have waited past every timeout and seen no certificate for height H. May they certify a different block at H?

No. A timeout changes local round state only. The lock still binds: without a proof-of-lock-change at H for a different value at a strictly later round, a validator prevotes its locked value. If a certificate for the locked block eventually appears, it is accepted then.

A node is restarted from a backup and cannot prove which messages it already signed. What does it do?

It must not sign for any (height, round, type) it cannot prove it has not already signed. Lost memory is treated as a signing fault, not as a fresh start, because double-signing is the one objectively punishable offence and the one that can break safety.

Why is publishing data without a proof not an acceptable way to unblock settlement?

It would create an accepted record before anything justifies it, break the rule that data and proof live in one transaction, and turn a safe halt into an unverified history. The design has no data-first path by construction, and no recovery may introduce one.

Is a PoS confirmation above the last accepted checkpoint final?

No. It is valid but provisional: after a settlement stall, anyone may start a bonded, delayed recovery that replaces history above the last checkpoint accepted on Ethereum and permanently retires the discarded heights, so the replaced range can never be certified or landed again. Accepted checkpoints and accepted batches are never touched, and a rolled-back transaction is not replayed — it must be resubmitted.

What does a recovery cost an invoker whose attempt is cancelled?

The whole bond. If any valid batch extending the current checkpoint is accepted during the delay, the recovery is cancelled and both the non-refundable component and the refundable balance go to the protocol treasury, so starting a recovery is never free. And even a completed recovery keeps the non-refundable component: every attempt, successful or not, costs it, and a cooldown follows each attempt.

A validator is locked on a block in a range that a completed recovery discards. Is it stuck, and must it halt?

No. Locks at retired heights are void. The validator is free to participate in the new history from resumeHeight onward, the old locked value is not evidence of misbehaviour, and it is not a reason to halt. Only a genuinely conflicting pair of signed messages is evidence, and a late certificate for a discarded height is usable as evidence of what was signed, not as a claim on canonical history.

A discarded branch is re-proven under the new recovery generation. Can it land?

No. The generation counter only invalidates proofs that were already generated; it is not what protects the boundary. A batch must start at max(lastLandedHeight + 1, resumeHeight): a start below resumeHeight is rejected at every generation, by anyone, while a batch that starts exactly at resumeHeight is the correct, landable case — it is the start the equality requires whenever lastLandedHeight + 1 ≤ resumeHeight. The acceptance rule is about where a batch starts, not which proof was used.

Assumptions

  • Fewer than one third of each epoch’s voting power is malicious, and the rest follows the lock and vote-uniqueness rules.
  • Enough validators are online to form quorums in normal operation; the protocol has no degraded mode when they are not.
  • Somebody keeps the data for every certified block until its proof lands.
  • At least one prover finishes each batch before the retention assumption expires.
  • The recovery trigger, bond split, delay, escalation, cooldown and cancellation values are unmeasured placeholders, and the argument that they cannot be abused rests on stated conditions rather than on measurement.
  • The non-refundable bond component is at least the value at risk inside the provisional range (B_REC_KEEP ≥ value_at_risk(D_MAX)), a sizing rule whose value is unmeasured.
  • The reward pool is actually funded and the reward is not below the cost of landing; an empty or below-cost pool reaches the settlement-stall trigger with no adversary at all.
  • Outside L1-level censorship and the absence of any prover, a sub-threshold coalition cannot force the settlement-stall trigger.
  • Ethereum itself keeps producing, including and finalizing blocks.
  • The timeout values, the retention window and the cap are placeholders awaiting measurement.
