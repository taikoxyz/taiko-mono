When things go wrong

This lesson gives you the failure-handling rules: what happens when a proposer hides a block, when votes or certificates do not arrive, when validators go offline, when data cannot be found or when settlement stalls — and the guarantee class that follows: a confirmation above the last batch accepted on Ethereum is provisional. A settlement stall halts the chain safely, and one timelocked governance action can clear it: during the stall the DAO may queue a single entry, and after the entry's stored deadline anyone may execute it once to discard the provisional range above the checkpoint and resume production. Nothing obliges governance to act, so a stall it does not act on halts the chain; nothing at or below the last accepted checkpoint changes, and value at or below it still exits on Ethereum alone through a withdrawal root of k distinct-family attestations funded over data that is still retrievable. This is a design description; none of it is implemented, measured, audited or deployed.

The idea

Failures are ordinary inputs to this protocol, not exceptions bolted on afterwards. Two rules do most of the work.

A timeout is never evidence of absence. When a timer expires, a node may change what it does — retry, change the view, halt — but it may not conclude that a vote, a certificate or a block does not exist somewhere else, and it may not invalidate anything (STATUS-10). A certificate that is revealed after everyone gave up is still the valid certificate.

A validator’s lock keeps two histories from both being certified. A validator that precommits a block at a height is locked on it. It will not prevote a conflicting block at that height unless it sees a proof-of-lock-change at the same height and a strictly later round (CONS-04). A certified height implies more than a third of the voting power is honest and locked, so a conflicting certificate would need those validators to break the lock — which is exactly the behaviour the rule forbids and slashing punishes.

A confirmation above the last batch accepted on Ethereum is provisional. The L2 certificate is real, and more than two thirds of the epoch’s voting power signed it; but until the batch carrying it is accepted on L1 with its proof, no v1 rule makes it permanent, and v1 has no path that replaces it. That is the guarantee class of every confirmation above the checkpoint, and every interface that shows one must say so (STATUS-04, STATUS-11).

A settlement stall halts the chain, and the halt is safe. A stall means that no batch extending the current L1 checkpoint has been accepted for long enough that a normal slow proof, or a single prover failure inside the normal envelope, cannot be the explanation. When it holds, the chain stops making progress. Everything at or below the last accepted checkpoint is preserved exactly; every confirmation above it stays provisional. One rule can discard that provisional range and restart production: the stall resolution — a DAO-queued entry, a stored deadline, one execution by any account. Nothing obliges governance to queue or execute it, and no other rule replaces the range.

A user can always leave from the last settled state. While the chain is halted, a withdrawal whose signal is already inside the last accepted checkpoint stays executable on Ethereum alone: it needs no new L2 blocks, no quorum and no validator. The release is proven against a withdrawal root — k attestations from k distinct backend families over the already-accepted checkpoint, each verified on L1, one verification per attestation — and the delay runs on the L1 clock from the root record’s own l1BlockNumber, with an unexpired veto able to add up to T_VETO (MEM-15, MSG-03). Forming the root is not free: the k−1 further proofs are off-chain work that a funded proving market must produce from the checkpoint’s still-retrievable data and witness, so if that market or the data is absent, no root forms and the exit waits, unboundedly (MEM-15(2b)). That exit is what keeps a halted chain safe for holders. Value above the last accepted checkpoint, or value that was never messaged, has no L1-provable claim while the chain is halted; it becomes withdrawable only when a later checkpoint covers it.

One rule can clear a stall, and it is not automatic. The stall resolution is the only route: during the stall the DAO may queue a single entry, the entry stores its own deadline, and after that deadline anyone may execute it once, which discards the whole provisional range above the last accepted checkpoint and lets production resume from the checkpoint. Nothing at or below the checkpoint is touched. Nothing obliges governance to queue or execute an entry, so a stall it does not act on leaves the chain halted with no protocol-level bound. Value above the checkpoint is discarded rather than replayed, and inclusion is not guaranteed: a published record that is outstanding across the halt can be discharged as dead by its own deadline rather than included, and no rule bounds the wait (lesson 11, ECON-11).

Locks are scoped by generation. A lock constrains votes at its height within the generation it was taken in, and it is released only by the rules above. An executed stall resolution begins a new generation, so a lock formed under the superseded generation cannot constrain votes under the new one, and no lock can wedge the chain through a restart. A certificate carrying a superseded generation would be void at the acceptance rule, though it remains admissible as evidence of what its signers signed (CONS-04, CONS-12).

A cohort that goes silent takes production with it. Within a set version nothing changes: the committed weights still include validators that have stopped signing, so if enough of them stop that quorum cannot be met, production halts — there is no degraded threshold and no replacement set for a version already committed. The L1 heartbeat changes who is in future versions: a cohort that stops attesting is excluded, once its coverage ends, from the versions judged against later windows, so those versions are drawn from the entries that keep attesting and quorum is reachable there. A cohort that keeps attesting and withholds its votes keeps its weight and stays in them: a heartbeat is a declaration of presence on L1, not proof of participation. The silent validators keep their whole bonded stake either way; they forfeit only the rewards they did not earn. The halt inside an already-committed epoch ends with no protocol change when the committed cohort of the epoch containing the next unfinalised height returns to vote, and it is permanent only while that cohort never does: that epoch is already committed and its roster immutable, and no v1 rule draws a replacement set for it. Changing the set, the schedule or the mechanism is what would need a protocol update, not the cohort's return.

The halt can still be permanent. Halting remains the correct action for missing data at or below the accepted checkpoint, for a missing validator-set commitment, for an L1 view that disagrees with consensus, and for a failure of the stake assumption. In those cases no rule-legal action can restore progress, and a halt can be permanent (HALT-01).

Situation → correct action

How it works

Withholding: what can be hidden, and what it costs

The design assumes messages can be late, lost or delivered selectively. For each object that can be withheld, the honest action is fixed (WH-01). No case of non-receipt justifies concluding that an object does not exist, and no case of non-receipt alone justifies slashing.

What is evidence, and what is only suspicion

A protocol penalty needs objective, self-contained, on-chain-verifiable evidence: two signed messages of the same type at the same height and round with different block ids, or a signed message that violates a rule whose inputs are all on-chain (WH-02). Everything else — a message that did not arrive, a height that did not advance, a proof that has not been submitted, a peer that did not answer — is local suspicion. It can change local behaviour: retry, view change, peer selection, disclosure. It cannot trigger a penalty or a statement about what exists elsewhere. Silence is not provable.

The lock, stated precisely

A validator’s lock is a local triple (locked_height, locked_value, locked_round). It is per height: it constrains votes at that height only, it survives a restart while the height is unresolved, and it is released in exactly two ways:

  • the locked value commits at that height within the current generation — once the value the validator locked on commits, the lock has done its work; or
  • the validator sees a proof-of-lock-change at the same height H, for a different value, at a round strictly later than its locked_round and earlier than the current round.

Nothing else unlocks. A proof-of-lock-change at the same round, at an earlier round, or for the value already locked does not unlock; a proof-of-lock-change at another height neither unlocks this height nor creates a lock there. The pol_round field carried in a proposal is only a hint; the validator must verify the proof itself. And a validator that holds a lock at a height and then sees a valid commit certificate for a different block at that height has evidence that the assumptions failed: it halts, and it does not treat the certificate as an unlock or as a reason to abandon the halt. an executed stall resolution begins a new generation, so a lock formed under the superseded generation is not superseded by the restart itself; a certificate carrying a superseded generation is void at the acceptance rule, and the lock remains in force until the ordinary rules release it.

Why this protects safety: a commit certificate requires more than two thirds of the epoch’s voting power. If a second, conflicting commit certificate existed, the two signer sets would overlap in more than one third of the power, and those validators would have had to sign conflicting blocks at the same height. The lock rule says the honest ones never do. A certificate that exists but has not been published can delay a node that has not seen it; it cannot displace a decision.

Halt: what it is, and what it costs

A correct validator halts — stops signing and stops advancing its certified view — when any of the following holds and no rule-legal action can resolve it (HALT-01):

  • it cannot obtain the data for a certified or locked block;
  • it cannot obtain a quorum for a height within the timeout ladder;
  • the L1 facts it must consume are not yet Ethereum-final, or its L1 view disagrees with the consensus view;
  • the L1 validator-set commitment for the epoch it is about to enter is absent; or
  • the unsettled-depth cap would be exceeded.

Halting is not a fork, not a rollback and not a vote to abandon the chain. A halting node keeps serving what it has and keeps its state so it can resume. There is no emergency key and no operator override. The one runtime governance action is the stall resolution — a DAO-queued entry, a stored deadline, one execution by any account — and it clears only a settlement stall: funds at or below the last accepted checkpoint are safe, the exit works, and a stall governance does not act on leaves the chain halted (HALT-04). If progress needs information that no rule-legal action can obtain, the protocol says so and stops.

Halting remains the correct outcome for a settlement stall and for a missing ingredient that no action can replace — anything at or below the last checkpoint accepted on Ethereum.

A settlement stall, step by step

  1. Production stops. No batch extending the current L1 checkpoint is accepted for long enough that a normal slow proof or a single prover failure cannot be the explanation. Nothing declares the stall early from a local view: the condition is a fact about the checkpoint, which every observer reads from Ethereum.
  2. Halt. Correct validators stop signing and keep their state and the data they hold. The chain serves what it has. Nothing at or below the last accepted checkpoint changes, and every confirmation above it stays provisional.
  3. Exit while halted. A withdrawal whose signal is at or below the last accepted checkpoint stays executable on Ethereum alone, subject to the withdrawal delay on the L1 clock and to a withdrawal root over that checkpoint: k distinct backend families must attest it, and the k−1 further proofs are off-chain work a funded proving market must produce from data that is still retrievable (MEM-15(2b)). Value above the checkpoint, or value that was never messaged, has no L1-provable claim until a later checkpoint covers it.
  4. The one route out. The stall resolution can clear the stall: the DAO queues a single entry, its deadline is stored in the entry, and after that deadline anyone may execute it once to discard the range above the checkpoint and resume production. There is no automatic route and no permissionless recovery, and the design gives no protocol-level guarantee that governance will act.
  5. What is preserved. The last accepted checkpoint and everything at or below it are never rewritten; the recovery generation is carried in the signed vote and header bytes and advances only through an executed stall resolution, so no height is retired. An executed resolution discards the whole range above the checkpoint — wholesale, never selectively — and a transaction in it is not replayed, so its inclusion is not guaranteed.

What a stall means for users

  • Below the accepted checkpoint, funds are not lost, and exit needs no L2 liveness. A withdrawal whose signal is at or below the checkpoint stays executable on Ethereum alone, subject to the withdrawal delay on the L1 clock and to a withdrawal root that k distinct backend families must attest; the k−1 further proofs must be funded and their data still retrievable (MEM-15(2b)). A halt does not forfeit the claim, but it can delay the root.
  • Above the accepted checkpoint, a confirmation stays provisional. No rule makes it permanent; the only rule that discards it is an executed stall resolution, which discards the whole range and resumes from the checkpoint. The resolution’s window is a notice window for value at or below the checkpoint, not an exit guarantee for value above it, and the resolution creates no offence for anyone. It is not settled, and value above the checkpoint has no L1-provable claim until a later checkpoint covers it.
  • Accepted history stays valid. Anything at or below a checkpoint or batch accepted on L1 is never touched by any rule.
  • Off-chain losses are not compensated. A trade, a signed message or a position built on a provisional confirmation has no protocol remedy (ECON-11).
  • Validators are not punished for halting. Halting is the required behaviour when the rules say so, and a lock that the ordinary rules release is not treated as misconduct.
  • Operators must not improvise. Publishing data without a proof accepts nothing — publication writes no batch state — so it cannot unstick the chain; picking a branch, by contrast, converts a safe halt into an unsafe divergence. No operator action clears a stall: the only route is the DAO-queued, consumed stall resolution.
  • A halt below the checkpoint may still be permanent. If every holder of an accepted block’s data has disappeared, nothing in the protocol can reconstruct it, and no rule may replace accepted history. That is a disclosed liveness cost of permanent settlement.
  • A stalled chain resumes only if governance acts. The stall resolution is the one route back, and nothing obliges governance to queue or execute an entry, so there is no protocol-level bound on how long a halt lasts. The heartbeat rule does not change this: it filters future set versions and cannot replace the roster of an epoch already committed.

Resuming a node afterwards

After a halt, a correct participant resumes only from the highest certified block whose data and certificate it can independently reconstruct (HALT-02). It never adopts an alternative history at a height it has already certified, and it never treats a timeout during the halt as evidence that a certificate does not exist. On resuming four rules apply:

  1. Each validator enforces a signed-message uniqueness check over its own signing history: no message of the same type at the same height and round may be signed twice, including after a restart or a resync. A validator that cannot prove it has not already signed for a (height, round, type) must not sign — lost memory is treated as a signing fault, not as a fresh start.
  2. A resuming validator re-verifies the epoch and the validator set for the height it is resuming at instead of trusting its cache.
  3. Replayed historical messages are rejected: every vote carries its epoch and round, and receiving rules reject messages for heights already certified.
  4. A resumed node’s first act is a data-and-certificate sync, not a proposal.

A node-level resync is not a rewrite of settled history: it starts from the last checkpoint accepted on Ethereum and resumes from there, and HALT-02’s rules govern what a node may do at heights it has already certified. No rule discards an unsettled height except the executed stall resolution, which discards the whole unsettled range above the checkpoint and nothing certified; no rule rewrites a certified one.

Why the chain halts instead of discarding unsettled history

An unbounded halt is not free: if settlement stops and nothing restarts it, the chain stops serving users, and the only remedies are outside the protocol. The design keeps the settled part settled and makes the unsettled part explicitly provisional. A checkpoint or batch accepted on Ethereum is never rewritten, and the history above it is discarded only by an executed stall resolution, which replaces the whole provisional range and nothing accepted; without one the history simply is not settled, and the halt continues until one runs. That is the trade: a strict boundary at the last accepted checkpoint, paid for with a liveness cost that no v1 rule removes.

The honest costs are disclosed, not priced away. Transactions above the checkpoint are not settled and their inclusion is not guaranteed; whether the chain resumes at all is left to governance queueing an entry and someone executing it, and the design states no protocol-level guarantee of liveness after a stall. Nothing lets any party choose a state, a checkpoint or a height — the resolution names nothing and discards the whole range — and nothing prices the halt with a bond.

A lock, walked through. A validator is locked on block B at (H, round 2). At round 5 it receives a proposal for a different block B′ whose hint says pol_round = 4. The hint is not evidence: the validator must obtain and verify the proof-of-lock-change itself. If that proof is for B′ at (H, round 4), it unlocks and prevotes B′, because 4 is strictly later than its locked round 2 and the value differs. If the proof is for round 2, or for B, it stays locked and prevotes B. If instead it obtains a commit certificate for a different block at H, it halts — it does not treat the certificate as an unlock. v1 never begins a new generation, so no restart can supersede the lock; it stays in force until the rules above release it, and the conflicting certificate remains evidence that the assumptions failed.

A prover that never finishes, and then a stall. A batch is certified at time t. At t + 30 minutes there is still no proof. Nothing special happens yet: a proof consuming the whole envelope is normal, not misconduct; other provers may produce a proof for the same data commitment and land it, and the first valid landing wins. If in addition no batch extending the current checkpoint is accepted for long enough that a normal slow proof cannot explain it, the chain stalls. Correct validators stop signing and keep serving what they hold. One rule can clear the stall: during it the DAO may queue a single entry, and after the entry's stored deadline anyone may execute it once, discarding the whole range above the checkpoint and letting production resume from the checkpoint. While it is halted, a user whose withdrawal signal is at or below the last accepted checkpoint can still exit on Ethereum alone, subject to the L1 withdrawal delay and to a withdrawal root that k distinct backend families must attest from data that is still retrievable. An executed resolution discards the range above the checkpoint wholesale and replays nothing; the certified batch and the transactions in it are unsettled and their inclusion is not guaranteed, and if governance never queues an entry or no one executes it, the halt persists.

What this gives you

  • A fixed, minimal response for every class of missing message or data, without inventing rules at run time.
  • Safety that does not depend on seeing a certificate: the lock protects the decision, not the publication.
  • A principled resume: a node restarts from the highest certified block whose data and certificate can be reconstructed, never from a competing branch.
  • A settled prefix that is never rewritten: history above it is provisional, and no v1 rule replaces or discards it.
  • A recovery generation that cannot be steered: it is a signed field that scopes certificates, v1 never advances it, and no state, checkpoint or height can be chosen through it.
  • An exit that needs no L2 liveness: a withdrawal whose signal is at or below the last accepted checkpoint is executable on Ethereum alone through a withdrawal root of k distinct-family attestations, subject to the funded proving market and to the data still being retrievable (MEM-15(2b)).
  • Locks scoped by generation: a lock constrains votes at its height within the generation it was taken in, and only an executed stall resolution begins a new one.
  • A clear account of silence: an entry that stops signing keeps its stake and its place in every committed set version, a validator that stops attesting loses its slots in future versions until it attests again, and a cohort that keeps attesting while withholding its votes halts production rather than shedding weight.

What it does not give you

  • Availability. If data for a block at or below the accepted checkpoint is gone, the halt can be permanent; retention is an assumption, not an enforced duty.
  • Permanent confirmations above the last accepted checkpoint. A certified block there is provisional: no rule makes it permanent, and an executed stall resolution discards it; off-chain losses are not compensated.
  • Guaranteed liveness after a stall. The stall resolution is the one way to clear one, and it is not automatic: a DAO queue and a permissionless execution are both required, nothing obliges either, and there is no protocol-level bound on how long a halt lasts.
  • A way to choose what to discard. The stall resolution discards the whole provisional range above the checkpoint — it cannot touch accepted history, name a subset or choose winners — and without it a stalled chain waits: transactions above the checkpoint are not settled, their inclusion is not a latency guarantee — a published record can be discharged as dead by its own deadline — and a party that needs one must be prepared to resubmit it later (lesson 11).
  • Punishment for silence. Only conflicting signed messages are objectively provable; a node that never answers is not slashable, and a validator that goes quiet keeps its stake and its place in every committed set version. Attestation is a selection filter, not a penalty: a lapse costs slots in future versions and no stake.
  • A way to keep producing after quorum is lost, and no rule that takes weight from anyone. Production halts; the committed set does not change, nothing removes the silent weight from it, and no v1 rule draws a replacement set for it. A cohort that keeps attesting while withholding its votes keeps its weight in the versions that follow.
  • An instant or operator unstick button. The only route is the timelocked stall resolution — a DAO queue plus a stored deadline plus one permissionless execution — and it is not an operator action; there is no emergency key and no bond.
  • Any measured detection rate, timeout value or retention window. Those are not yet measured.

Check yourself

Validators have waited past every timeout and seen no certificate for height H. May they certify a different block at H?

No. A timeout changes local round state only. The lock still binds: without a proof-of-lock-change at H for a different value at a strictly later round, a validator prevotes its locked value. If a certificate for the locked block eventually appears, it is accepted then.

A node is restarted from a backup and cannot prove which messages it already signed. What does it do?

It must not sign for any (height, round, type) it cannot prove it has not already signed. Lost memory is treated as a signing fault, not as a fresh start, because double-signing is the one objectively punishable offence and the one that can break safety.

Is publishing a batch’s data to L1 without a proof a way to unblock settlement?

No. Publication is permissionless, but it writes a publication record and no batch state: it accepts nothing and advances no height, so settlement resumes only when a valid proof lands. A publication that is never proven before its deadline is dead — derived from its own stored block number and the registered deadline, with no status field — and the proof discharges it as dead by one comparison, needing neither its bytes nor any execution. Re-publication is the remedy for a user whose record expired: the same data can be published again as a new record with a fresh clock, so publication never becomes a second acceptance path. A due record is resolved in the proof as dead, void or executed. Dead is tested first and unconditionally: a record whose own deadline has passed at the anchored view is dead whatever its contents. Otherwise, while the record is live, the walk reads the record’s transactions in the record’s own order, each one either runs or is set aside at its turn; a live record all of whose transactions are set aside is void, and otherwise the record is executed.

Is a PoS confirmation above the last accepted checkpoint final?

No. It is valid but provisional: no rule makes it permanent, and the one rule that discards it is an executed stall resolution, which discards the whole provisional range. Accepted checkpoints and accepted batches are never touched. Value above the checkpoint has no L1-provable claim until a later checkpoint covers it, and no protocol penalty compensates an off-chain loss.

What clears a settlement stall?

The stall resolution. During the stall the DAO may queue a single entry; the entry stores its own deadline; after that deadline anyone may execute it once, which discards the whole range above the last accepted checkpoint and resumes production from the checkpoint. It is not automatic and nothing obliges governance to act, so there is no protocol-level guarantee of liveness after a stall. Nothing at or below the checkpoint is touched, and the exit from that state stays available on Ethereum alone.

Does a lock survive a generation change?

An executed stall resolution begins a new generation, so a lock formed under the superseded generation cannot constrain votes under the new one; otherwise the lock stays in force at its height until the commit of the locked value or a strictly later proof-of-lock-change releases it. A certificate carrying a superseded generation is void at the acceptance rule, which is what the signed generation scopes.

The chain has stalled. Does anything restart it?

Only the stall resolution can: a DAO-queued entry, a stored deadline, one execution by any account. Nothing automatic and nothing permissionless restarts the chain, and nothing obliges governance to act, so the chain stays halted until an entry is queued and executed, with no protocol-level bound on how long that takes. A user can still exit from the last settled state on Ethereum alone while the halt lasts.

What is the recovery generation for?

It is a counter carried in the signed vote and header bytes that scopes certificates, locks and uniqueness: a certificate carrying a superseded generation is void at the acceptance rule, so a branch scoped to an older generation could never be accepted again. The generation advances only through an executed stall resolution — once per entry — and no height is retired; a certificate at or below the last accepted checkpoint is judged under the generation in its own header.

A third of the voting power stops signing. Is the chain stuck?

For the set version already committed the silent weight still counts, so quorum can fail and production halts, and no v1 rule replaces that committed roster. What changes is the next versions: if the cohort stops attesting on L1, it is excluded, once its coverage ends, from the versions judged against later windows, and those versions are drawn from the entries that keep attesting, so quorum is reachable there. If the cohort keeps attesting and only withholds its votes, it keeps its weight and can stall again. Nothing is taken from the silent validators either way: a lapse costs future slots and never stake, and clearing a halt inside an already-committed epoch still needs the cohort to return; the stall resolution clears a settlement stall, not a quorum-loss halt.

Assumptions

  • Fewer than one third of each epoch’s voting power is malicious, and the rest follows the lock and vote-uniqueness rules.
  • Enough validators are online to form quorums in normal operation, and honest L1 heartbeat transactions are included inside their windows, one per window of HEARTBEAT_WINDOW L1 blocks. Inside a set version the protocol has no degraded mode and no rule removes a validator that goes quiet, so a silent cohort halts production; the heartbeat excludes a cohort that stops attesting from future versions only, and an adversary that censors honest heartbeats can exclude honest validators (F8).
  • Somebody keeps the data for every certified block until its proof lands, or publishes it to L1 while the blobs are retrievable.
  • At least one prover finishes each batch before the retention assumption expires.
  • The stall resolution is the one route out of a settlement stall: a DAO-queued entry, a stored deadline, one execution by any account. It is not automatic, nothing obliges governance to act, and there is no protocol-level bound on how long a halt lasts; the exit from the last settled state stays available on Ethereum alone.
  • The reward pool is actually funded and the reward is not below the cost of landing; an empty or below-cost pool reaches a settlement stall with no adversary at all.
  • Outside L1-level censorship and the absence of any prover, a sub-threshold coalition cannot force a settlement stall.
  • Ethereum itself keeps producing, including and finalizing blocks.
  • The timeout values, the retention window and the cap are placeholders awaiting measurement.
