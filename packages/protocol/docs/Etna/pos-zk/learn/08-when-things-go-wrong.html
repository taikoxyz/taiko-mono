When things go wrong

This lesson gives you the failure-handling rules: what happens when a proposer hides a block, when votes or certificates do not arrive, when validators go offline, when data cannot be found or when settlement stalls — and the guarantee class that follows: a confirmation above the last batch accepted on Ethereum is provisional. A settlement stall halts the chain safely, and it restarts only if governance queues and executes a timelocked, resume-only decision from the last accepted checkpoint. This is a design description; none of it is implemented, measured, audited or deployed.

The idea

Failures are ordinary inputs to this protocol, not exceptions bolted on afterwards. Two rules do most of the work.

A timeout is never evidence of absence. When a timer expires, a node may change what it does — retry, change the view, halt — but it may not conclude that a vote, a certificate or a block does not exist somewhere else, and it may not invalidate anything (STATUS-10). A certificate that is revealed after everyone gave up is still the valid certificate.

A validator’s lock keeps two histories from both being certified. A validator that precommits a block at a height is locked on it. It will not prevote a conflicting block at that height unless it sees a proof-of-lock-change at the same height and a strictly later round (CONS-04). A certified height implies more than a third of the voting power is honest and locked, so a conflicting certificate would need those validators to break the lock — which is exactly the behaviour the rule forbids and slashing punishes.

A confirmation above the last batch accepted on Ethereum is provisional. The L2 certificate is real, and more than two thirds of the epoch’s voting power signed it; but until the batch carrying it is accepted on L1 with its proof, the protocol’s own rules can discard it when the chain restarts from that checkpoint. That is the guarantee class of every confirmation above the checkpoint, and every interface that shows one must say so (STATUS-04, STATUS-11).

A settlement stall halts the chain, and the halt is safe. A stall means that no batch extending the current L1 checkpoint has been accepted for T_STALL_GOV — long enough that a normal slow proof, or a single prover failure inside the normal envelope, cannot be the explanation. The condition is measured on L1, so no participant’s local view can declare it early (GOV-04). When it holds, the chain stops making progress, and nothing restarts it automatically or permissionlessly. Everything at or below the last accepted checkpoint is preserved exactly; every confirmation above it stays provisional and may be discarded by a restart.

A user can always leave from the last settled state. While the chain is halted, a withdrawal whose signal is already inside the last accepted checkpoint stays executable on Ethereum alone: it needs no new L2 blocks, no quorum and no validator, only the withdrawal delay measured on L1 (MEM-15). That exit is what makes a slower restart tolerable. Value above the last accepted checkpoint, or value that was never messaged, has no L1-provable claim while the chain is halted; it becomes withdrawable only when a later checkpoint covers it.

Restarting takes a governance decision, and the decision is narrow. When the chain has stalled past the threshold, governance may queue a restart under a timelock T_GOV_RESUME, long enough that every user can exit from the last settled state first. Once queued, the decision cannot be shortened. It is resume-only: the chain restarts from the latest checkpoint accepted on Ethereum and discards everything above it — not a certified subset, not a height governance prefers, and not a state governance picks. Governance chooses no state, no checkpoint and no height; all three come from L1 state alone. Once the timelock has elapsed and the entry is not void, any account may execute it, and the executor chooses nothing either.

Progress voids the decision. If a batch extending the checkpoint is accepted before the queued decision executes, the action is void, and any account may cancel the queued entry. A certificate or a proof for a discarded height is not progress, because neither is accepted settlement. The restart resumes from the last L1-accepted checkpoint and touches nothing at or below it.

When the restart executes, everything above the accepted checkpoint is discarded. Inclusions, ordering, balances and bridge messages that were only provisional cease to be part of the chain, and the recovery generation advances. The generation is carried in the signed vote and header bytes: validators sign the new generation, and certificates carrying the old generation are void at the acceptance rule (L1-06). A transaction in the discarded range is not replayed automatically: it is lost, it must be resubmitted, and its inclusion is not guaranteed. Off-chain losses are not compensated by any protocol penalty (ECON-11).

Locks taken in the discarded range belong to the old generation. A validator that was locked on a block above the accepted checkpoint is not stuck when the chain restarts: a lock constrains votes only within the generation it was taken in, so it does not bind the validator in the new generation, it is not evidence that the validator misbehaved, and it is not a reason to halt. Without that, a locked third of the power could wedge the new chain. A late certificate for a discarded height is likewise void for canonical purposes, though it remains admissible as evidence of what its signers signed (CONS-04, CONS-12).

A cohort that goes silent loses its place at the boundary, not its stake. Within a set version nothing changes: the committed weights still include validators that have stopped signing, so if enough of them stop that quorum cannot be met, production halts — there is no degraded threshold. Selection into a future set version is different. To be in it, a validator must post a periodic attestation on Ethereum, a heartbeat. An entry that has not attested within HEARTBEAT_WINDOW ending at the version’s commit point is excluded from the root — not decayed, not slashed, not confiscated — and keeps its whole bonded stake. At the next boundary the remaining eligible weight becomes the whole of the epoch’s voting power, so the quorum predicate is reachable again without any rule removing weight, and the L1-time rotation (CONS-16) draws a stalled chain’s restart set from the eligible validators. A validator that forgot to attest loses its slot until it attests again; re-attesting restores eligibility for the following boundary. The costs are a periodic Ethereum transaction (anyone may submit it, and a relayer may batch signatures into one), a second heartbeat key, and the slot itself when an operator lapses.

Where no restart can help, the chain still halts. Halting remains the correct action for missing data at or below the accepted checkpoint, for a missing validator-set commitment, for an L1 view that disagrees with consensus, and for a failure of the stake assumption. In those cases no rule-legal action can restore progress, and a halt can be permanent (HALT-01).

Situation → correct action

How it works

Withholding: what can be hidden, and what it costs

The design assumes messages can be late, lost or delivered selectively. For each object that can be withheld, the honest action is fixed (WH-01). No case of non-receipt justifies concluding that an object does not exist, and no case of non-receipt alone justifies slashing.

What is evidence, and what is only suspicion

A protocol penalty needs objective, self-contained, on-chain-verifiable evidence: two signed messages of the same type at the same height and round with different block ids, or a signed message that violates a rule whose inputs are all on-chain (WH-02). Everything else — a message that did not arrive, a height that did not advance, a proof that has not been submitted, a peer that did not answer — is local suspicion. It can change local behaviour: retry, view change, peer selection, disclosure. It cannot trigger a penalty or a statement about what exists elsewhere. Silence is not provable.

The lock, stated precisely

A validator’s lock is a local triple (locked_height, locked_value, locked_round). It is per height: it constrains votes at that height only, it survives a restart while the height is unresolved, and it is released in exactly two ways:

  • the locked value commits at that height within the current generation — once the value the validator locked on commits, the lock has done its work; or
  • the validator sees a proof-of-lock-change at the same height H, for a different value, at a round strictly later than its locked_round and earlier than the current round.

Nothing else unlocks. A proof-of-lock-change at the same round, at an earlier round, or for the value already locked does not unlock; a proof-of-lock-change at another height neither unlocks this height nor creates a lock there. The pol_round field carried in a proposal is only a hint; the validator must verify the proof itself. And a validator that holds a lock at a height and then sees a valid commit certificate for a different block at that height has evidence that the assumptions failed: it halts, and it does not treat the certificate as an unlock. The discarded-range case is the exception: after a restart a certificate from the old generation is void at the acceptance rule, the lock does not bind the new generation, and the validator is neither stuck nor required to halt.

Why this protects safety: a commit certificate requires more than two thirds of the epoch’s voting power. If a second, conflicting commit certificate existed, the two signer sets would overlap in more than one third of the power, and those validators would have had to sign conflicting blocks at the same height. The lock rule says the honest ones never do. A certificate that exists but has not been published can delay a node that has not seen it; it cannot displace a decision.

Halt: what it is, and what it costs

A correct validator halts — stops signing and stops advancing its certified view — when any of the following holds and no rule-legal action can resolve it (HALT-01):

  • it cannot obtain the data for a certified or locked block;
  • it cannot obtain a quorum for a height within the timeout ladder;
  • the L1 facts it must consume are not yet Ethereum-final, or its L1 view disagrees with the consensus view;
  • the L1 validator-set commitment for the epoch it is about to enter is absent; or
  • the unsettled-depth cap would be exceeded.

Halting is not a fork, not a rollback and not a vote to abandon the chain. A halting node keeps serving what it has and keeps its state so it can resume. There is no emergency key and no operator override; the only way the chain moves again after a stall is the timelocked, resume-only restart, and only when its own rule allows it (HALT-04). If progress needs information that no rule-legal action can obtain, the protocol says so and stops.

A pure settlement stall is different: it has a rule-legal way forward, the timelocked restart. Halting remains the correct outcome when the missing ingredient is data or participation that no restart can replace — anything at or below the last checkpoint accepted on Ethereum.

Clearing a stall, step by step

  1. Trigger. One condition starts the process: a settlement stall — no batch extending the current L1 checkpoint has been accepted for T_STALL_GOV. The threshold is strictly larger than the proof envelope, so a normal slow proof or a single prover failure cannot trigger it. It is measured on L1, and no participant’s local view can declare it early.
  2. Halt. Correct validators stop signing and keep their state and the data they hold. The chain serves what it has. Nothing at or below the last accepted checkpoint changes, and every confirmation above it stays provisional and may be discarded by a restart.
  3. Exit while halted. A withdrawal whose signal is at or below the last accepted checkpoint stays executable on Ethereum alone, subject only to the withdrawal delay on the L1 clock (MEM-15). Value above the checkpoint, or value that was never messaged, has no L1-provable claim until a later checkpoint covers it.
  4. The decision. Governance may queue a restart under the timelock T_GOV_RESUME, long enough that every user can exit before it executes. Once queued, the timelock cannot be shortened. The decision is resume-only: it may not choose a state, a checkpoint or a height, and nothing at or below the accepted checkpoint may be touched. Once the timelock has elapsed and the entry is not void, any account may execute it, and the executor has no discretion: the effect is fixed by the rule from L1 state alone.
  5. Void on progress. If a batch extending the checkpoint is accepted before the queued decision executes, the action is void, and any account may cancel the queued entry. A certificate or a proof for a discarded height is not progress, because neither is accepted settlement.
  6. Effect. The chain restarts from the latest checkpoint accepted on Ethereum and discards everything above it. The restart advances the recovery generation, which is carried in the signed vote and header bytes, so validators sign the new generation and certificates from the old generation are void at the acceptance rule. A transaction in the discarded range is lost: it is not replayed automatically, it must be resubmitted, and its inclusion is not guaranteed.
  7. Locks in the discarded range. A lock belongs to the generation it was taken in. A validator locked on a discarded block is not stuck, the old locked value is not evidence of misbehaviour, and it is not a reason to halt. A late certificate for a discarded height is void for canonical purposes but remains admissible as evidence of what was signed.
  8. Afterwards. The chain resumes from the last accepted checkpoint (lastLandedHeight + 1) under the new generation. Nothing restarts automatically and there is no permissionless path: if governance does not queue and execute the decision, the chain stays halted. That dependency is stated as a trust assumption, not as a guarantee.

What a stall means for users

  • Below the accepted checkpoint, funds are not lost, and exit needs no L2 liveness. A withdrawal whose signal is at or below the checkpoint stays executable on Ethereum alone, subject only to the withdrawal delay on the L1 clock (MEM-15). A halt does not forfeit it.
  • Above the accepted checkpoint, a confirmation can be discarded. A restart resumes from the last checkpoint accepted on Ethereum and discards everything above it. A transaction in the discarded range is lost: it is not replayed automatically and must be resubmitted.
  • Accepted history stays valid. Anything at or below a checkpoint or batch accepted on L1 is never touched by a restart.
  • Off-chain losses are not compensated. A trade, a signed message or a position built on a provisional confirmation has no protocol remedy (ECON-11).
  • Validators are not punished for halting. Halting is the required behaviour when the rules say so, and a lock in the discarded range does not stick.
  • Operators must not improvise. Publishing data without a proof accepts nothing — publication writes no batch state — so it cannot unstick the chain; picking a branch, by contrast, converts a safe halt into an unsafe restart. The only legitimate restart is the timelocked, resume-only decision, and there is no operator unstick button.
  • A halt below the checkpoint may still be permanent. If every holder of an accepted block’s data has disappeared, nothing in the protocol can reconstruct it, and a restart may not replace accepted history. That is a disclosed liveness cost of permanent settlement.
  • A stalled chain resumes only if governance acts. There is no automatic or permissionless path, and no protocol-level bound on how long a halt lasts. This is a social-layer trust assumption, stated as one.

Restarting afterwards

After a halt, a correct participant resumes only from the highest certified block whose data and certificate it can independently reconstruct (HALT-02). It never adopts an alternative history at a height it has already certified, and it never treats a timeout during the halt as evidence that a certificate does not exist. On restart four rules apply:

  1. Each validator enforces a signed-message uniqueness check over its own signing history: no message of the same type at the same height and round may be signed twice, including after a restart or a resync. A validator that cannot prove it has not already signed for a (height, round, type) must not sign — lost memory is treated as a signing fault, not as a fresh start.
  2. A resuming validator re-verifies the epoch and the validator set for the height it is resuming at instead of trusting its cache.
  3. Replayed historical messages are rejected: every vote carries its epoch and round, and receiving rules reject messages for heights already certified.
  4. A resumed node’s first act is a data-and-certificate sync, not a proposal.

A restart is not a rewrite of settled history: it restores the last checkpoint accepted on Ethereum and resumes from there, and HALT-02’s rules still govern what a node may do at heights it has already certified. A node must not treat a discarded height as certified, and a lock taken in the discarded range neither binds it nor obliges it to halt.

Why unsettled history can be discarded instead of halting forever

An unbounded halt is not free: if settlement stops and nothing restarts it, the chain stops serving users, and the only remedies are outside the protocol. The design keeps the settled part settled and makes the unsettled part explicitly provisional. A checkpoint or batch accepted on Ethereum is never rewritten; history above it can be discarded by a timelocked, resume-only governance decision that resumes from the checkpoint, and the generation advance makes certificates from the discarded range void. That is the trade: a defined way back to a working chain, paid for with a weaker guarantee above the last accepted checkpoint and with a restart that governance must choose to make.

The honest costs are disclosed, not priced away. Transactions in the discarded range are lost and must be resubmitted, and whether the chain resumes depends on governance queueing and executing the decision: there is no protocol-level guarantee of liveness after a stall. Governance can choose when the decision executes, but not what it does — the resume point, the discarded range and the new generation are fixed by L1 state and the rule — and a batch accepted before execution voids the decision entirely.

A lock, walked through. A validator is locked on block B at (H, round 2). At round 5 it receives a proposal for a different block B′ whose hint says pol_round = 4. The hint is not evidence: the validator must obtain and verify the proof-of-lock-change itself. If that proof is for B′ at (H, round 4), it unlocks and prevotes B′, because 4 is strictly later than its locked round 2 and the value differs. If the proof is for round 2, or for B, it stays locked and prevotes B. If instead it obtains a commit certificate for a different block at H, it halts — it does not treat the certificate as an unlock. If the chain has since restarted from the checkpoint, however, the lock belongs to the old generation: the validator does not halt on the old value and takes part in the new history from the resume point onward.

A prover that never finishes, and then a stall. A batch is certified at time t. At t + 30 minutes there is still no proof. Nothing special happens yet: a proof consuming the whole envelope is normal, not misconduct; other provers may produce a proof for the same data commitment and land it, and the first valid landing wins. If in addition no batch extending the current checkpoint is accepted for T_STALL_GOV, the settlement-stall trigger is met. The chain halts: correct validators stop signing and keep serving what they hold, and nothing restarts it automatically or permissionlessly. While it is halted, a user whose withdrawal signal is at or below the last accepted checkpoint can still exit on Ethereum alone, subject only to the L1 withdrawal delay. Restarting requires governance to queue a timelocked decision; the decision is resume-only, so the chain restarts from the last checkpoint accepted on Ethereum and every block above it — including the certified batch and any transaction in it — is discarded. The restart advances the recovery generation: validators sign the new generation, and certificates from the old generation are void at the acceptance rule. A transaction in the discarded range is lost and must be resubmitted; nothing replays it.

What this gives you

  • A fixed, minimal response for every class of missing message or data, without inventing rules at run time.
  • Safety that does not depend on seeing a certificate: the lock protects the decision, not the publication.
  • A principled restart: resume from the highest certified block whose data and certificate can be reconstructed, never from a competing branch.
  • A settled prefix that is never rewritten and a defined way to restart above it, so a settlement stall has a rule-legal route back when governance acts.
  • A restart that cannot choose history: it resumes from the last accepted checkpoint, discards everything above it wholesale, and advances the generation so old-generation certificates are void at the acceptance rule.
  • An exit that needs no L2 liveness: a withdrawal whose signal is at or below the last accepted checkpoint is executable on Ethereum alone (MEM-15).
  • Locks that do not survive a restart: a validator locked on a discarded block is not wedged, and the old locked value is not treated as misbehaviour.
  • A liveness rule that takes nothing from anyone: a validator that stops attesting is excluded from future set versions, keeps its stake, and returns by attesting, so a quorum lost to silence is recoverable at a boundary.

What it does not give you

  • Availability. If data for a block at or below the accepted checkpoint is gone, the halt can be permanent; retention is an assumption, not an enforced duty.
  • Permanent confirmations above the last accepted checkpoint. A certified block can be discarded when the chain restarts from the checkpoint, a discarded transaction is lost and must be resubmitted, and off-chain losses are not compensated.
  • Guaranteed liveness after a stall. Clearing a stall depends on governance queueing and executing the timelocked decision; there is no bond, reward or permissionless path that substitutes for it, and no protocol-level bound on how long a halt lasts.
  • A selective restart, or a route back for the discarded range. The restart resumes from the checkpoint and discards the contiguous range above it wholesale: governance has no choice about which transactions inside that range survive, so the action cannot be used to pick winners. That is a property of the wholesale discard, not a promise about the outcome — discarded transactions are not replayed automatically and their inclusion is not guaranteed, so a party whose transaction was discarded must resubmit it.
  • Punishment for silence. Only conflicting signed messages are objectively provable; a node that never answers is not slashable, and a missed heartbeat is not an offence either. A validator that goes quiet loses its place in future set versions, not its stake.
  • A way to keep a set version producing after quorum is lost, and no rule that takes weight from anyone. Production halts rather than continuing at a lower threshold; the way back is at the boundary, where validators that have not posted a heartbeat are excluded from the next set version and the eligible remainder becomes the whole quorum denominator. No stake is reduced or taken, and re-attesting restores eligibility.
  • An instant or operator unstick button. A settlement stall is cleared only by the timelocked, resume-only decision, and only when governance queues and executes it — not by an operator, an emergency key or a bond.
  • Any measured detection rate, timeout value, stall-resolution parameter or retention window. Those are not yet measured.

Check yourself

Validators have waited past every timeout and seen no certificate for height H. May they certify a different block at H?

No. A timeout changes local round state only. The lock still binds: without a proof-of-lock-change at H for a different value at a strictly later round, a validator prevotes its locked value. If a certificate for the locked block eventually appears, it is accepted then.

A node is restarted from a backup and cannot prove which messages it already signed. What does it do?

It must not sign for any (height, round, type) it cannot prove it has not already signed. Lost memory is treated as a signing fault, not as a fresh start, because double-signing is the one objectively punishable offence and the one that can break safety.

Is publishing a batch’s data to L1 without a proof a way to unblock settlement?

No. Publication is permissionless, but it writes a publication record and no batch state: it accepts nothing and advances no height, so settlement resumes only when a valid proof lands. A publication that is never proven before its deadline is discarded and can be published again, so it never becomes a second acceptance path.

Is a PoS confirmation above the last accepted checkpoint final?

No. It is valid but provisional: if the chain stalls and governance executes a restart from the last checkpoint accepted on Ethereum, the confirmation is discarded with everything else above that checkpoint. Accepted checkpoints and accepted batches are never touched, and a discarded transaction is not replayed — it must be resubmitted.

What clears a settlement stall?

A timelocked, resume-only governance decision. It restarts the chain from the last accepted checkpoint and discards everything above it; it cannot choose a state, a checkpoint or a height, and once queued it cannot be shortened. If a batch extending the checkpoint is accepted first, the decision is void and any account may cancel the queued entry. Nothing restarts automatically or permissionlessly, and there is no bond and no reward in the path.

A validator is locked on a block in a range that a restart discards. Is it stuck, and must it halt?

No. The lock belongs to the generation it was taken in, and the restart begins a new generation, so the validator is free to participate in the new history from the resume point onward. The old locked value is not evidence of misbehaviour and is not a reason to halt. Only a genuinely conflicting pair of signed messages is evidence, and a late certificate for a discarded height is usable as evidence of what was signed, not as a claim on canonical history.

The chain has stalled and governance has not acted. Does anything restart it?

No. There is no automatic or permissionless restart. The chain stays halted until governance queues and executes the timelocked, resume-only decision, or until settlement makes progress on its own — in which case any queued decision becomes void and any account may cancel it. That liveness dependency on governance is a disclosed trust assumption, not a protocol guarantee. A user can still exit from the last settled state on Ethereum alone while the halt lasts.

What makes a certificate from before a restart unusable?

The restart advances the recovery generation, which is carried in the signed vote and header bytes. Validators sign the new generation, and a certificate carrying the old generation is void at the acceptance rule. The generation advance is what stops a discarded branch from being accepted again.

A third of the voting power stops signing and never posts a heartbeat. Is the chain stuck?

Not permanently, and nothing is taken from the silent validators. For the set version already committed the silent weight still counts, so quorum can fail and production halts for that epoch. To be selected into a later set version an entry must attest on Ethereum within HEARTBEAT_WINDOW; the silent entries are excluded from the next committed root, the eligible remainder becomes the whole of the epoch’s voting power, and the quorum predicate is reachable again. The excluded validators keep their stake, are not slashed, and become eligible again by attesting. The L1-time rotation (CONS-16) can close a stalled epoch early and draw the restart set from eligible validators.

Assumptions

  • Fewer than one third of each epoch’s voting power is malicious, and the rest follows the lock and vote-uniqueness rules.
  • Enough validators are online to form quorums in normal operation, and enough post their L1 heartbeats that each future set version can be populated. Inside a set version the protocol has no degraded mode; at a boundary, validators that have not attested are excluded from the next version while keeping their stake.
  • Somebody keeps the data for every certified block until its proof lands, or publishes it to L1 while the blobs are retrievable.
  • At least one prover finishes each batch before the retention assumption expires.
  • The stall trigger T_STALL_GOV and the timelock T_GOV_RESUME are unmeasured placeholders. The timelock is long enough that every user can exit from the last settled state before a restart executes, and governance cannot shorten it once the decision is queued.
  • Clearing a stall depends on governance queueing and executing the decision. This is a social-layer trust assumption with no protocol-level bound: if governance does not act, the chain stays halted. There is no bond, reward or permissionless path to restart it. A captured or coerced governance can also act opportunistically within the rule, waiting for a genuine stall and letting an unfavourable provisional range be discarded; the narrow effect, the timelock and the exit window mitigate this without removing it.
  • The reward pool is actually funded and the reward is not below the cost of landing; an empty or below-cost pool reaches the settlement-stall trigger with no adversary at all.
  • Outside L1-level censorship and the absence of any prover, a sub-threshold coalition cannot force the settlement-stall trigger.
  • Ethereum itself keeps producing, including and finalizing blocks, and includes honest heartbeat transactions. If honest heartbeats can be censored on L1, validators that are online can still be excluded from future set versions — the named falsifier of the exclusion argument, which is Open.
  • The timeout values, the retention window, the cap, the stall threshold and the timelock are placeholders awaiting measurement.
