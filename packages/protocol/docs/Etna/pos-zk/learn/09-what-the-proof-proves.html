09 — What the proof proves — and what it cannot

Problem this lesson solves. It states in plain language what the ZK proof establishes about a batch, why the validator set and the predecessor must be derived from L1 rather than supplied by the prover, why one certificate per batch is enough, and which sentence the proof must never be used to support.

In plain language

One guest, three claims. A single guest program proves the conjunction of three things for one batch (PRF-01): W1, that the batch's head block carries a valid commit certificate over (chain id, epoch, height, round, block id) signed by more than two thirds of the voting power of the epoch's validator set, with that set authenticated from L1 state and every epoch boundary crossed accompanied by an authenticated set transition; W2, that executing the batch's blocks in the order the consensus chain fixed, starting from the predecessor state root, yields the claimed successor state root; and W3, that the data it executed is the data the accepting L1 transaction publishes (PRF-07). Acceptance requires all three.

Public inputs are what the verifier checks the proof against; the witness is what the prover supplies. The guest commits to a canonical journal whose field list and order are normative (PRF-02). For every field that carries authority, the L1 verifier contract does not accept a caller-supplied value: it derives the value itself from L1 state or from the transaction, and it recomputes the Fiat–Shamir challenge rather than trusting the prover's. The witness contains the evidence the guest must fully constrain — validator entries with Merkle proofs against the committed set root, per-signer signatures and the signer bitmap, the header chain, epoch-transition evidence, the batch's transaction data and the blob field elements (PRF-03).

Why the validator set must come from L1. If the set were prover-supplied, a prover could hand in a set it controls together with matching signatures, and the proof would verify a finality claim about a chain no independent validator ever saw — sound in isolation, meaningless in fact. PRF-03 therefore states that no witness element may define the authoritative validator set: a set is accepted only if its recomputed commitment equals the set root that L1 state supplies, never the proof payload. PRF-13 generalises this: any value a prover could otherwise choose freely — the set, the quorum threshold, the predecessor, the data commitment, the challenge point, the configuration hash, the program identity — must be derived by the contract, recomputed inside the guest from a contract-derived value, or checked by an L1 precompile. A binding that consists only of "the caller passed it" is not a binding (PRF-13). And nothing computed by the host is trusted: values entering the guest through reads, hints or unconstrained regions are unverified assertions until an explicit check constrains them (PRF-11).

What the guest checks for the head certificate (in order). Every signature verifies under the corresponding public key from the authenticated set, over the canonical vote bytes for the domain tag, chain id, epoch, height, round and block id; signers are distinct, with one bitmap bit per distinct signer and no bit outside the set; the effective stake of the signers is strictly greater than the quorum threshold; the threshold is the minimal integer q with 3q > 2 · totalVotingPower, computed with the same rounding rule as the L1 contract so a guest/contract mismatch is impossible; the total equals the sum of the authenticated entries; and the head block hash equals the hash of the reconstructed head header, whose parent chain links without gaps back to the predecessor block hash the contract already accepted (PRF-04). Epoch boundaries get their own checks (PRF-05), and execution is checked block by block against each header's committed state root (PRF-06).

Why only the head block's certificate is verified. The guest verifies the commit certificate of the head block only; every other block in the batch is covered by the header hash chain back to the predecessor checkpoint, and no ancestor certificate is verified on-chain or in-guest (PRF-08). The argument is the one a light client uses: under the commit rule a validator precommits a block only after validating it and its parent relation, and a certificate needs more than two thirds of the voting power, so at least one third of the power is correct and locked; a certificate for the head therefore implies the head is the unique finalized block at its height, and hence that the whole prefix it commits to is the unique finalized prefix. The premises are named: fewer than one third of the epoch's voting power is Byzantine, correct validators obey vote uniqueness and the lock rule, and the set is L1-authenticated. If a premise fails, the argument fails with it, and the proof does not detect that (PRF-08, CONS-12).

The sentence the proof must never be used to say: "the proof verifies, therefore no hidden competing chain exists." A ZK proof does not establish that. It establishes that the evidence presented is sufficient, under the stated consensus assumptions, for the claimed transition from an authenticated predecessor to a claimed successor. If at least one third of the epoch's voting power violates the consensus assumptions, two conflicting certificates can both be cryptographically valid and both can produce accepting proofs. ZK verification does not repair failed consensus assumptions (PRF-08, the proof-statement page's opening warning, 01 §6.5).

What that leaves. The proof is evidence about one claimed transition, not a survey of the world. Among the things it does not establish: that no competing finalized chain exists; that the batch's data is retrievable by others; that the L2 chain will continue; that an honest validator behaved correctly off-chain; and that the guest program itself is correct (PRF-01–PRF-13 and the "what the proof does not establish" table on the proof-statement page).

Precise definitions

  • Statement. The three-part claim W1 (consensus finality), W2 (execution) and W3 (data binding) that one guest proves for one batch; acceptance requires all three (PRF-01).
  • Public input / journal. The canonical, ordered list of values the guest commits to and the verifier checks against; the L1 contract derives the authoritative fields itself (PRF-02).
  • Private witness. The evidence the prover supplies — set entries with inclusion proofs, signatures and bitmap, header chain, transition evidence, transaction data, blob field elements — none of which may define the authoritative validator set (PRF-03).
  • Validator-set root. The canonical commitment to the epoch's set; the accepted value comes from L1 state, and a set is accepted only when its recomputed commitment equals that root (PRF-03).
  • Quorum threshold. The minimal integer q with 3q > 2 · totalVotingPower, computed with the same rounding rule on both sides (PRF-04, CONS-03).
  • Head-certificate-only verification. One certificate verified per batch; the rest of the batch is covered by header linkage back to the previously accepted checkpoint (PRF-08).
  • Host trust and enforced bindings. Host-computed values are unverified assertions until constrained, and accelerated primitives are trusted only as far as the circuits constrain them (PRF-11); a caller-supplied value is not a binding (PRF-13).
  • Acceptance policy. The Inbox accepts a proof from any registered verifier whose program identity is accepted for the epoch's configuration; whether one backend or two are required is recorded as an open launch decision (PRF-09).

The counterfactual row is the whole lesson in one line: with a prover-chosen set and a prover-chosen total, every signature check in the guest can still pass, and the proof still means nothing about this chain. That is why the journal's authoritative fields are contract-derived, why the guest is required to recompute the commitment of any set it uses, and why a host-supplied value is treated as an unverified assertion until it is constrained (PRF-02, PRF-03, PRF-11, PRF-13).

Attacks this prevents

  • A prover-asserted validator set or quorum. Authority comes from L1 state, so a set the prover controls cannot be substituted together with matching signatures (PRF-02, PRF-03).
  • Asserted-not-enforced bindings. Anything the prover could otherwise choose freely must be derived, recomputed or precompile-checked (PRF-13).
  • Host-controlled inputs. Values entering the guest from outside are unverified until explicitly constrained, including key recovery, point validity and set membership (PRF-11).
  • Signature-counting errors. Distinct signers, one bitmap bit each, stake summed from the authenticated entries, and a threshold computed identically on both sides (PRF-04).
  • A proof reused for another batch. The range, predecessor, epoch, set root and data commitment are in the statement, so a proof for a different batch cannot substitute (L1-10, PRF-02).
  • Overclaiming uniqueness. Naming the limit stops a verifier from reading an internally consistent certificate as a claim about the whole network (CONS-12).

Attacks this does not prevent

  • One third or more Byzantine stake, compromised keys or a client that breaks the lock rules. Then two conflicting certificates can both verify and both produce accepting proofs; ZK verification does not repair failed consensus assumptions (PRF-08, CONS-12).
  • A soundness bug in an accepted backend or in the guest program. That is a bridge-theft-class failure the protocol cannot repair; the acceptance policy is recorded as open (PRF-09).
  • Data unavailability. The proof binds data content; retrievability is a network property governed by the retention rules (DA-05).
  • A halt. Liveness is conditional and stated separately; the proof says nothing about whether the chain continues (LIVE-01).
  • Off-chain honesty. Only signed statements are provable; a validator that withheld data without signing a conflicting message has left no proof of misconduct (ECON-04).

Self-check

Which journal field, if it were caller-supplied, would let a prover fabricate a finality claim?

The validator-set root and the total voting power (with the quorum threshold derived from them). A prover that could assert its own set and total could satisfy every signature check with keys it controls; that is why the contract derives these values from L1 state (PRF-02, PRF-03).

Why is verifying only the head block's certificate sufficient — and when is it not?

The batch's headers link by parent hash back to the already-accepted checkpoint, and the head's certificate plus the lock rule implies the head's prefix is unique — under the premises: fewer than one third Byzantine, correct validators obeying vote uniqueness and the lock rule, and an L1-authenticated set. If a premise fails, the argument fails with it and the proof does not detect that (PRF-08).

A proof verifies. Has the network agreed that no other finalized history exists?

No. Two conflicting certificates can both be cryptographically valid and both produce accepting proofs if at least one third of the epoch's power violates the consensus assumptions. Any uniqueness claim is relative to an L1-authenticated checkpoint and those assumptions (CONS-12, 01 §6.5).

Challenge this. The lesson used: (i) A-CONS-1 per epoch; (ii) correct validators obeying CONS-02 and CONS-04; (iii) the set being L1-authenticated, including the cross-epoch lock carry-over that CONS-09 marks assumed-with-argument (review target F1); (iv) PRF-08's argument as stated, with its premises attached; (v) the guest and both backends implementing the same statement (PRF-09) and the program identity being bound by the verifier, not the prover (PRF-10); (vi) the illustrative stake unit being unmeasured. Falsifier: find a sentence in this lesson that reads a verifying proof as evidence that no competing finalized chain exists, or that drops one of PRF-08's premises.
