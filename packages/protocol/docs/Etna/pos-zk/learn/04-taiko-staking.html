04 — Why TAIKO must be at risk

Problem this lesson solves. It explains why a vote in this design only means something when it is backed by bonded TAIKO that can be taken away, and what the specification does and does not claim about concentration, penalties and rewards.

In plain language

A validator's vote is a promise about the future: "I will not sign a conflicting block at this height." A promise is only worth something if breaking it costs the promiser. So the protocol ties voting power to stake: TAIKO bonded in an L1 staking contract, active in the epoch's validator set. No eligible stake means the vote does not count, and the stake behind a vote can be slashed if the validator is shown to have broken a rule (INV-03).

Custody. The stake is the existing TAIKO ERC-20 held by an L1 staking contract. D7 fixes the denomination: staking and slashable collateral are TAIKO, gas stays ETH, and there is no replacement staking token (D7, MEM-01). The Phase 3 decision document names the existing L1 mainnet token address, 0x10dea67478c5F8C5E2D90e5E9B26dBe60c54d800; the rule itself says only "the existing token address", so the address is sourced from that document, not from the rule (04 — architecture decision §3).

Activation. Joining is objective and permissionless: bond TAIKO, bind an operator key, and enter the first-come activation queue (MEM-03). The activation delay is a derived quantity — at least one L1 finality depth plus the set-commitment lookahead — because the epoch's set must be fixed from L1 state before the epoch begins (ACTIVATION_DELAY in 09, CONS-13).

Exit. Leaving is also objective: an unbonding queue whose delay is derived from the whole settlement pipeline, not from proving time alone (MEM-05, ECON-03). The evidence window must be shorter than that delay, or a validator could unbond between the offence and the evidence (ECON-07).

Slashing follows stake at offence time. Exiting after an offence does not escape the penalty (MEM-06, INV-03). Penalties also require objective evidence: equivocation, a lock-rule violation, or another objectively encodable offence. A message that did not arrive is local suspicion, never misconduct (WH-02, ECON-04).

The penalty is convex. The specification's parameter table gives the correlated penalty as a fraction of stake of the form min(m · f, 1), with m chosen so that the penalty saturates when the implicated fraction f reaches the one-third safety threshold, and records Ethereum's m = 3 as the sourced form (ECON-05, 09 — correlated penalty). The consequence the project records is that the safety threshold and the confiscation threshold coincide (DECISIONS.md D-4, sourced).

A cap per address does not stop concentration. This is one of the project's explicit non-arguments: "per-address caps prevent monopolization" may not be used as evidence (01 — requirements and threat model §6.4). The specification states it as a non-claim (MEM-10, ECON-10). The reason is short: a cap limits labels, not capital. An attacker who wants a fraction b of the stake buys the same tokens and splits them over more addresses; the dominant cost, acquiring the tokens, is unchanged. The specification's rejected-alternatives entry reaches the same conclusion (LIM-02). No claim is made that one operator will not control many addresses, and the honest statement is that concentration is observable, not prevented.

Rewards need a funding decision. The specification gives a funding identity rather than a rate: rewards cannot exceed fees plus priority fees plus MEV plus an explicit subsidy, minus L1 data cost, L1 verification cost and bridge operations (ECON-02, ECON-12, 09 — reward rate). TAIKO has no mint path today. The specification records this in its limitations register and reaches the honest conclusion: rewards may be unfundable without a user decision — a treasury transfer or a token upgrade — and no token-appreciation assumption may substitute for funding (LIM-01, ECON-02).

Precise definitions

  • Effective stake / voting power. The bonded, active TAIKO that counts in an epoch's set; only that counts (MEM-02, glossary). A live token-balance read is not the authority for it.
  • No consensus without stake at risk. A key with no eligible stake cannot vote, and a vote counts only if the signer's stake is in the authenticated set (INV-03).
  • Custody. The L1 staking contract holds the existing TAIKO ERC-20; no new token, no ETH bonds (MEM-01, D7).
  • Activation. Objective, permissionless, first-come queue (MEM-03), with the delay of 09 — ACTIVATION_DELAY.
  • Exit delay. The unbonding queue's delay, derived from the complete settlement pipeline (MEM-05, ECON-03).
  • Slashing at offence time. The penalty follows the stake the validator held at the time of the offence (MEM-06).
  • Correlated penalty. A penalty that grows with the fraction of stake implicated, of the form min(m · f, 1), saturating at the one-third threshold (ECON-05).
  • Cap non-claim. A per-address cap is not an anti-concentration measure and the protocol does not claim it is (MEM-10, LIM-02).

Timing, with its inputs left visible. ACTIVATION_DELAY is at least one L1 finality depth plus the set-commitment lookahead (09). Ethereum's own finality is about two epochs; at 32 slots of 12 s that is 768 s ≈ 12.8 min (derived from sourced Ethereum configuration). One epoch here is a placeholder 300 L2 blocks = 600 s = 10 min (derived from an unmeasured parameter). So the floor is roughly 22.8 min (derived, with unmeasured inputs) before the extra depth F_L1 is added — and F_L1 is unmeasured. The full exit delay D_WITHDRAW is a sum of six terms — proof bound, detection, evidence submission, L1 inclusion, L1 finality, processing — every one of them unmeasured, and it must not be derived from nominal proving latency alone (MEM-05, 09 — D_WITHDRAW).

Attacks this prevents

  • Voting power without at-risk capital. A key with no eligible stake cannot vote and is not counted (INV-03).
  • The exit race. Unbonding between an offence and the evidence does not escape the penalty, because slashing follows the stake at offence time and the evidence window is shorter than the withdrawal delay (MEM-06, ECON-07).
  • Cheap coalition bribery. The correlated penalty makes the coalition's cost grow super-linearly with the implicated fraction and confiscates the implicated stake at the one-third boundary, so a bribe must compensate for a penalty that grows with the coalition (ECON-05).
  • False accusations. A penalty needs objective, self-contained, on-chain-verifiable evidence; silence is never proof of misconduct (WH-02).
  • The "cap protects us" illusion. MEM-10 and LIM-02 state plainly that a cap constrains labels, not capital, so no one can rely on it as an anti-concentration guarantee.

Attacks this does not prevent

  • An attacker who acquires or borrows enough TAIKO to reach one third. Whether the attack is unprofitable is the assumption A-ECO-1, which depends on market value at the time and must be re-evaluated; the protocol must not claim deterrence (A-ECO-1, LIM-01).
  • Concentration through liquid staking or lending markets: delegation is out of scope in v1, and the limitations register calls derivatives an unpriced attack surface (LIM-01).
  • Honest correlated-penalty accidents: on a small validator set, one operator running a duplicated key can trigger a correlated hit. The research records this as a real trade-off; its rates are unmeasured (research — economics §2.3).
  • Unfunded rewards. Since TAIKO has no mint path today, the reward identity may not balance without a user decision, and that is disclosed as a limitation rather than solved (LIM-01).
  • Compensation of users who lost funds: the specification states the scope of what is and is not compensated (ECON-11).

Self-check

Why is voting weight not read from the token balance at vote time?

Voting power is the effective stake of the epoch's authenticated set (MEM-02, MEM-08). A live balance read is not the authority for it — the project records that getVotes is not a stake oracle because votes can be renounced while the balance stays intact (DECISIONS.md D-4).

A validator exits one day after equivocating. Is it safe from slashing?

No. Slashing follows the stake held at the time of the offence, so exiting after the offence does not escape the penalty (MEM-06, INV-03).

Does a per-address cap stop an attacker from controlling a third of the stake?

No. A cap limits how many tokens one address may hold, not how many tokens one owner may buy. The acquisition cost is unchanged and the added overhead of splitting across addresses is additive; the specification records this as a non-claim (MEM-10, LIM-02).

Challenge this. The lesson relied on: (i) TAIKO having a market value and being obtainable (A-ECO-2) and the economic assumption A-ECO-1 that slashed value plus lost rewards exceeds attack profit — an assumption, never a guarantee; (ii) the correlated penalty formula being a fraction of implicated stake, with m = 3 tagged in 09 as a "sourced form" from Ethereum rather than a measured parameter of this design; (iii) the worked numbers (total voting power, the cap, the epoch length) being illustrations, since the specification fixes no such values; (iv) the exclusion of delegation in v1, which means the concentration analysis here does not cover liquid-staking derivatives; (v) rewards requiring a user funding decision because TAIKO has no mint path today (LIM-01). Falsifier: find a sentence in this lesson that claims an attack is unprofitable, or a penalty value that is presented as measured — both would be violations of GEN-01/GEN-04.
