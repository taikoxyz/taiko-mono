10 — Who pays, who earns, who loses

What a validator spends, where its ETH rewards come from, what has to be proven before any stake is taken, and why the minimum stake is an operator-economics number rather than a security threshold.

This is a design. Nothing on this page is implemented, deployed, audited or measured. Numbers are tagged derived (arithmetic on stated inputs), sourced (from an Ethereum constant or a published rule) or unmeasured (a value with no measurement behind it yet). A formula is a relation the design has to satisfy, not a value.

The idea

Two assets do two different jobs. TAIKO is what a validator bonds and can lose: it is the staking and slashable collateral asset, held in one L1 custody ledger. ETH is what the chain earns and spends: L2 execution fees are paid in ETH, L1 data and verification costs are paid in ETH, and validator rewards are denominated and paid in ETH. Gas stays ETH.

A reward is a nominal ETH amount fixed when it is awarded. The protocol says nothing about its fiat value, and no rule may be conditioned on TAIKO’s price rising. A reward schedule that only works if the token appreciates is an unfunded promise: TAIKO has no mint function today, so a reward funded by new TAIKO would need a token upgrade, and the reward pool can pay out only what has actually flowed into it.

A penalty is not a mood and not a discretion. Exactly two kinds of behaviour are objectively provable from signed messages, and only a proven offence consumes stake. The size of the penalty is a function of how much of the epoch’s voting power the offence implicated, so a single misbehaving validator pays a small amount while a coalition that approaches the one-third safety boundary loses everything it bonded for that epoch. Silence is not on the offence list: a validator that stops attesting keeps its stake and its place in every committed set version, and forfeits only the rewards it did not earn.

Exit is an ordered queue, not a switch. A validator that asks to leave keeps validating and stays fully slashable until its exit takes effect, and even then its stake is held until every evidence window it was exposed to has closed. The delay exists because the offence clock starts on L2, possibly long before the batch is provable, and publishing data to L1 before its proof settles nothing: the delay has to cover the whole path from offence to L1-applied penalty.

The money path

How it works

1. What a validator pays for

A validator runs a node and holds capital. The node costs are ETH: hardware able to execute a worst-case block inside the two-second cadence, bandwidth for gossip and block fetches, storage for L2 state plus the retention window, an Ethereum client of its own for L1 finality evidence, L1 gas for bonding, exiting, submitting evidence and posting the periodic L1 heartbeat, and the labour to operate all of it. The capital cost is the TAIKO it locks plus the opportunity cost of locking it for the duration of the unbonding delay.

Write C_op for the annual operating cost of one validator in ETH, S for the TAIKO it bonds, and ρ_capital for the annual ETH return it requires per unit of bonded TAIKO (risk-free rate plus an illiquidity premium for the unbonding delay, an expected-slashing-loss premium and a price-risk premium). The annual bill for n validators is n · (C_op + ρ_capital · S) ETH. Every term is unmeasured for this design: there is no operations study, no cost-of-capital observation and no measured node cost.

2. Where rewards come from

All ETH-denominated payouts in an accounting period must satisfy the funding identity

rewards_ETH(period) ≤ F_exec_L2 + F_priority_L2 + POOL_TOPUP
                        − C_L1_data − C_L1_verify − C_bridge_ops − D_subsidy

where the two F terms are L2 execution and priority fees actually collected, POOL_TOPUP is any other recorded L1-side inflow, the three C terms are L1 data, verification and bridge operating costs, and D_subsidy is the draw that capitalises a bounded, expiring subsidy account. The subsidy is a transfer of the protocol’s own inflow, not a second funding source: it can smooth timing inside its term and can never make an unfunded schedule funded. Ordering value that leaks to searchers or builders is not revenue and has no term in the identity.

Payment runs through the reward pool held by the L1 staking contract:

  • Participation is attested, not assumed. When land(data, proof) accepts a batch it writes the head certificate’s authenticated signer bitmap into the staking contract’s per-epoch participation accumulator. Nothing else may set a participation bit, and a payout is never made on presence in the set or on bonded balance alone.
  • Being selectable is a duty a validator renews on L1. A payout requires a signature that a landed batch attests through the signer bitmap, so a validator that does not sign earns nothing for the batches it did not sign. Membership in a future set version has an L1 condition: the entry must have posted a heartbeat inside the window containing that version’s commit point. Silence loses the unearned rewards, and a lapse costs slots in future versions and no stake: re-attesting restores the entry at a later commit point, and a cohort that keeps attesting while withholding its votes keeps its weight.
  • Each epoch has one allocation. Alloc(e) is fixed before the first claim and may not exceed the pool’s realised net inflow. The participant set and the participating stake PS(e) are frozen when the epoch’s evidence window closes; each signer receives floor(Alloc(e) · effStake / PS(e)), and floor-rounding remainders stay in the pool.
  • Claims are permissionless. Anyone may call claimValidatorReward(epoch, index); the payout goes to the entry’s recorded owner, once per (epoch, index). Claims open only after the epoch’s evidence window closes, because only then is the allocation fixed. A batch of epoch e that lands after that instant does not change PS(e); its validators are recorded as participants but are not paid from epoch e’s allocation.
  • The pool is not collateral. ETH in the pool is never stake, never counted as bonded balance, never slashable and never borrowed against.

L2 fee revenue reaches the L1 pool by one path. L2 execution and priority fees accumulate in an L2 fee vault, and a permissionless sweep moves them through the preserved Bridge to the pool, crediting the pool only when the Bridge has authenticated the message; the destination is a protocol constant, and the caller chooses only when to sweep. There is no protocol-mandated schedule and no keeper duty. The honest consequences are stated with the path: rewards lag the settlement pipeline, an empty pool pays nothing (Alloc(e) = 0), and a bridging failure stops rewards rather than creating a claim on future revenue (ECON-02 clause 7).

3. Why rewards cannot assume token appreciation

TAIKO has no mint function today: the supply was minted once and no inflation path exists on L1. A reward funded by new TAIKO therefore requires a token upgrade, which is a governance action, and it is not a standing funding source. Because rewards are paid in ETH, a TAIKO-denominated reward would additionally need a conversion rule and a price; no price constant is permitted anywhere in the design.

A reward is a nominal ETH amount fixed when it is awarded. No rule, parameter, user interface or learning page may assume, require, promise or be conditioned on an increase in TAIKO’s market price, market depth, float or pool value. “Rewards are sustainable” is a claim that holds only once fee-only inflow covers the validator bill; until then the honest description is that the schedule is reserve-dependent.

4. What can be slashed, and what evidence a penalty needs

The offence catalogue is closed. Only behaviour on the list consumes bonded stake, and only through slash(address v, uint epoch, bytes evidence), which any address may call.

OffenceWhat the evidence isWhat it costs
Equivocation — two conflicting signed messages by one validator at one (chain_id, height, round, type), where type is a proposal, a prevote or a precommit the two signed messages themselves, plus the validator index; each signature is checked against the key committed for the signer at the epoch the message names. The pair is self-authenticating: no witness, no peer receipt and no other validator’s cooperation is needed, and the submitter need not be a validator or hold any stake. an immediate debit, plus a correlated debit computed once the epoch’s evidence window closes
Lock-rule violation in the only form that is objectively provable: a conflicting signed pair at the same (height, round) the same kind of pair. Evidence is one canonical encoding of (domainTag, chainId, offenceType, payload), with a right-padded domain tag and the chain id first. A submission that fails any check reverts: no state is written, no balance is touched, no replay marker is set, and the submitter pays only L1 gas. The identity of an offence is (validator, epoch, offenceType, keccak256(evidence)); a second submission of the same identity is a no-op that pays no second bounty and does not extend any window.

The deadline is an L1 timestamp: evidence is admissible only if the block that includes the slash transaction has block.timestamp ≤ evidenceClose(epoch). The window opens when the epoch’s set root is committed and closes no earlier than the event that makes the offence observable plus the window length. W_EVIDENCE must cover the entire pipeline — permitted proving latency, L1 inclusion at a chosen quantile, L1 finality, detection, and submission — and it must satisfy the strict inequality W_EVIDENCE + T_PROCESS + CORR_DELAY + M_ev < D_WITHDRAW. Equality is non-conforming: it turns a deadline into an L1 ordering race. Every term is unmeasured.

5. Why penalties scale with implicated stake

For epoch e, each entry retains SlashBase(v, e), its effective stake in that epoch’s set version, and an offence at e is charged against that base, never against the balance at evidence time. Let f be the fraction of the epoch’s total voting power held by entries with an applied offence of the class. Then

c(f)     = min( (f / F_SAT) ^ CORR_Q , 1 )      with F_SAT = 1/3, CORR_Q in {1, 2}
P(v, e)  = min( SlashBase(v, e) · ( IMM_FRAC + c(f) ) , SlashBase(v, e) )
charge   = min( P(v, e) , bonded(v) , remaining_exposure(v, e) )

The correlated term c(f) is convex and saturates exactly at the one-third boundary: a lone offender implicates a tiny fraction and pays little, while a coalition holding a third of the epoch’s voting power pays 100% of its epoch stake in the correlated component, on top of the immediate component. CORR_Q = 1 reproduces Ethereum’s min(3f, 1); CORR_Q = 2 reproduces Polkadot’s min((3x/n)^2, 1) sourced. IMM_FRAC is a per-class fraction with 0 < IMM_FRAC ≤ 1, so every proven offence has a non-zero immediate cost; its value is unmeasured.

The immediate component is debited when the accepted evidence transaction executes. The correlated component cannot be known before the window closes, so it is computed once at evidenceClose(e) + CORR_DELAY and debited by the permissionless applyCorrelated(e, class). A debit is bounded by the bonded balance and by the per-epoch base; the applied amount is taken, the remainder is recorded in the event and is not carried as a debt outside the ledger.

The destination is fixed: every debited amount goes to the protocol treasury, except a reporter bounty that is strictly less than the total penalty actually debited, so that self-reporting is never profitable, and, when a correlated penalty is collected, a keeper fee of CORR_KEEPER_PPM of the amount collected paid to the caller. Such a collection has three named destinations: the caller’s keeper fee, the reporter bounty where a separate evidence submitter is entitled to it, and the remainder to the protocol treasury. There is no burn option and no burn share: the treasury is where penalties end up, not a burn address. The destination never changes the size of the penalty (ECON-06).

The honest tail. On a small validator set, a correlated penalty makes one operator’s duplicated key a correlated loss for a non-trivial fraction of the set. The mitigation is operational: slashing-protection databases, remote signers, disciplined key custody. No parameter choice removes the tail, and the correlated penalty is not harmless.

6. Exit and unbonding

Exit is a request plus a wait, and the wait has three gates.

  1. Request. The owner calls requestExit(). The entry stays in every set version it is already committed to, keeps voting, and remains fully slashable. Leaving the set immediately would silently shrink the validator set and would buy no protection at all.
  2. Effectiveness. The exit takes effect at the first set version whose snapshot point is at or after the request and which the per-epoch churn cap does not exclude. The cap limits how much voting power may go inactive in one epoch (CHURN_LIMIT · TotalVP); deferred exits are served in the objective order of their L1 request coordinates (blockNumber, txIndex, logIndex), are never cancelled by the protocol and are never overtaken. Only the owner’s cancelExit() before effectiveness removes one.
  3. Withdrawal. The owner may withdraw only when the exit has been effective for at least D_WITHDRAW, every evidence window for every epoch the entry was in has closed, and no slashing exposure remains. At any moment the owner may withdraw at most bonded − max_exposure, where max_exposure is the largest retained per-epoch charge base over epochs whose windows are still open.
D_WITHDRAW ≥ T_PROOF_MAX_PERMITTED + T_detect + T_evidence_submit
             + T_L1_include(p) + T_L1_final + T_PROCESS + M

T_L1_include(p) must be a quantile of L1 inclusion delay, never a mean and never a fixed slot duration. The delay may not be derived from nominal proving latency alone: the offence clock starts on L2, possibly long before the batch is provable, and data published to L1 before its proof settles nothing, so the window has to cover the whole pipeline. If the delay is judged too long, the answer is to reduce the pipeline terms it covers, never to set it below the formula. Every term is unmeasured.

7. Minimum stake is an economic question

The minimum bonded amount for one validator is fixed by the operator break-even relation, in ETH-revenue terms:

S_min ≥ C_op_annual / ( r_gross − ρ_ops )

where r_gross is the annualised ETH reward actually paid per unit of bonded TAIKO and ρ_ops is the operator’s required margin in the same unit. The relation is dimensionally closed and contains no price term; the TAIKO price enters only through the required margin, which is why that margin is time-varying rather than a constant. If the pool cannot pay r_gross > ρ_ops, the denominator is zero or negative and no finite minimum exists: entry is closed in practice and permissionless entry is unsatisfiable in that regime. The honest statement is that the design is unfunded, not that the minimum can be lowered until operators appear.

Attack cost is a different inequality and it does not produce a per-validator number. Aggregate stake must satisfy S_total ≥ Λ · (EV_extractable + OV_option) / (b · P(t) · (1 + σ(t))), where b is the Byzantine threshold, P(t) the TAIKO price, σ(t) the slippage and illiquidity premium for acquiring the stake, and EV_extractable and OV_option the value extractable during the attack window and the option value of holding the ability to attack. No individual S appears, so no rule may derive a per-validator minimum from it. The minimum must also be at or below what a median intended participant can afford, or permissionless entry is permissionless only in name. Borrowed and leveraged stake changes the cost floor to a financing cost, and the protocol cannot observe leverage; per-address caps constrain labels, not capital. Every value here is unmeasured, and no figure may be copied from Bitcoin or Ethereum validator costs, whose workloads differ.

What this gives you

  • A reward that is a nominal ETH amount with a stated funding identity, not a yield promise tied to a token price.
  • Penalties that require objective, self-authenticating evidence, that any address can submit, and that credit no one’s discretion.
  • Fixed penalty destinations: the protocol treasury receives the remainder of every debit, beside a reporter bounty strictly below the total penalty and, when a correlated penalty is collected, a keeper fee of CORR_KEEPER_PPM to the caller. No burn option exists: the treasury is where penalties end up, not a burn address.
  • Proportional cost: the more stake an offence implicates, the larger the fraction of each offender’s stake that is taken, saturating at the one-third safety boundary.
  • A withdrawal gate that makes it impossible to release stake before the window that could charge it has closed.
  • A minimum stake derived from what it costs to run a validator, and an aggregate attack-cost bound that is stated as a separate inequality.
  • A set version fixed on L1 two epochs ahead and drawn from the entries that attested on L1 in the window containing its commit point, so selectability is renewed by the validator’s own L1 act — and a lapse costs future slots, never stake.

What it does not give you

  • No reward from an empty pool: L2 fees must be swept from the L2 fee vault through the preserved Bridge, rewards lag the settlement pipeline, and a bridging failure stops rewards instead of creating a claim on future revenue.
  • No compensation to users. A penalty debits an offender’s stake and does nothing else: it does not restore discarded history, return a bridge message or cover a loss, and there is no universal bound on user losses.
  • No penalty for being offline, for censoring a transaction or for omitting it. Those are liveness or inclusion questions, not offences: a validator that stops signing keeps its stake and its place in every committed set version, and no stake is taken from it. A lapse in the L1 heartbeat is not an offence either; it only removes the entry from the versions committed while it is not covered.
  • No deterrent guarantee. The deterrence argument is conditional on slashed stake having value and being confiscated, and it can fail with no rule changing if the market moves.
  • No protection from the correlated-penalty tail, in which one operator’s key mishap costs honest peers.
  • No liveness-discounted membership: no rule takes stake or a committed place from a validator that goes quiet. But membership in a future version is renewed by an L1 heartbeat, so a validator that stops attesting is absent from the versions committed while it is not covered, and a cohort that keeps attesting while withholding its votes can still halt production instead of being replaced. No v1 rule draws a replacement set for a version already committed.
  • No measured cost, revenue, reward or market figure. There are no operations study, fee history or liquidity data behind any value on this page.

Check yourself

A validator is offline for a day and misses its rounds. Is it slashed?

No. Being offline, voting late and timing out are not offences; the only slashable consensus behaviour is objectively signed conflict. The validator loses the rewards it did not attest — no participation bit, no payout — and it keeps its stake and its place in every committed set version. If it also misses a heartbeat window, it is absent from the versions committed in that window and re-attesting restores it at a later commit point; if enough of the committed set stops signing, quorum can fail, production halts rather than continuing at a lower threshold, and v1 has no rule that clears the halt or removes the silent weight from a committed version. The withdrawal gate still applies to stake already exposed to an open epoch.

Two validators equivocate in the same epoch. Why is the second offence’s marginal cost much larger than the first’s?

Because the correlated component is a convex function of the fraction f of the epoch’s voting power implicated by the class, not a fixed fee per offender. One offender sets a small f and pays a small correlated fraction; as f approaches one third, each additional offender raises the multiplier for the whole class until it saturates at 100%. The immediate component is charged per offence and is bounded by the same per-epoch base, so once the base is exhausted further offences are recorded but not collected.

The reward pool holds 500 ETH. Can a validator count on a share of it?

Only through the stated identity. The pool pays Alloc(e) per epoch, and Alloc(e) may not exceed the pool’s realised net inflow and is subject to the frozen participant set and participating stake. A large balance smooths timing; it does not create an obligation, and no rule may pay out more than has flowed in or borrow against the pool. Fee revenue must first be swept from the L2 fee vault through the preserved Bridge; until it arrives, the pool has no inflow and nothing is payable at all.

Assumptions

  • Fewer than one third of the voting power in each epoch is malicious; the deterrence and safety arguments stop at that boundary.
  • TAIKO keeps enough market value and liquidity that confiscating it is a real loss, and that acquiring a third of it is expensive. Both are market facts the protocol does not control and cannot observe on-chain.
  • L2 fee revenue accumulates in the L2 fee vault and is swept through the preserved Bridge to the pool; a bounded and recorded top-up may add to it, and otherwise an empty pool pays nothing.
  • Operators use slashing protection and disciplined key custody, because the correlated penalty makes one duplicated key expensive for others.
  • The evidence window’s close is anchored to the epoch’s set-root commit. The L1 fact that would tie the close to the epoch’s production is not fixed, so a settlement halt longer than the window can make a later epoch’s offences inadmissible.
  • Ethereum keeps producing blocks, including and finalizing them; the whole settlement and evidence path is denominated in L1 time, and honest heartbeat transactions are included inside their windows. A lapse costs future slots and not stake, and censoring honest heartbeats can exclude honest validators from future versions (F8).
