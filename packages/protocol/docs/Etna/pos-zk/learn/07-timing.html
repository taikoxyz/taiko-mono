Two seconds and thirty minutes

This lesson gives you the timing model of the design: what two seconds buys, what thirty minutes is for, when data is actually on Ethereum, and which status a user is looking at at each point. It is a design description: nothing here is implemented, measured, audited or deployed.

The idea

The design produces one L2 block every two seconds. That number is a production cadence. It is not how long a block takes to become certified, not how long a proof takes, not when a batch reaches Ethereum, and not when a withdrawal becomes available. Those are five separate clocks, and the most common way to misread this protocol is to collapse them into one.

A batch is a contiguous range of L2 blocks that is accepted on Ethereum by one transaction carrying two things together: the batch data, and a validity proof over it (L1-01). There is no state on Ethereum in which the data has arrived and the proof has not, or the reverse. One transaction, both objects, one checkpoint advance.

While a batch is being proven, its data is not on Ethereum. It exists on L2 participants’ machines — the validators and full nodes that hold the blocks and serve them — until the batch is accepted in the L1 transaction that carries the data and the proof together. Only from that transaction onward is the canonical copy of the data on Ethereum.

The timeline

How it works

A block every two seconds

Heights advance by one every two seconds in the design’s operating assumptions. For each height the protocol selects a proposer by weighted hash, validators execute and vote, and the height becomes certified when more than two thirds of the epoch’s voting power precommits it (CONS-05). A height is certified on its own; there is no long confirmation window. Above the latest checkpoint accepted on Ethereum that certification is provisional. If a round does not complete, the round budget — a ladder of, for example, 3, 6, 12, 24, 30 seconds — moves the proposal to the next round (CONS-07). Certification latency is therefore a small number of round trips, seconds at the proposed round budget, and it is not yet measured at the intended validator count and geography.

What a batch is, and when it exists

Batches are not created by consensus. Blocks are certified one at a time; a batch is the range that somebody proves and lands. Any account may do this, and the first valid submission wins (L1-04). Three structural rules shape the choice of range:

  • The range must be contiguous and must start exactly at max(lastLandedHeight + 1, resumeHeight): one block after the last accepted batch, and never at or below a height retired by a completed recovery (L1-06). Landing is strictly in order, and a discarded range can never be landed.
  • The batch length is bounded by data, not by preference. At the target rate one 2-second L2 block is one 131,072-byte blob (EIP-4844, sourced); EIP-7691 allows 6 blobs per L1 block as the target and 9 as the maximum (sourced); and the batch’s data and its proof must travel in one transaction (L1-01). A batch is therefore at most about 6 blocks at the target rate, and 9 blocks is the hard ceiling: the bound is BATCH_BLOCKS × blobs_per_block ≤ 9, with 6 the sustainable value (derived from the sourced constants).
  • Failed verification leaves no partial effect: the checkpoint write happens only after the verifier returns true (L1-03).

Pipelining: proving keeps pace with production

Proving a batch takes far longer than producing its blocks, so batches overlap. While batch n is being proven, the blocks of batches n+1 and n+2 are already being produced and certified, and independent provers work on them in parallel. How many proofs must be in flight is a queueing identity: L / Δ, where L is the proof-latency bound and Δ the batch’s time span. With a 30-minute bound and the largest sustainable batch of 6 blocks (12 seconds of history), that is about 150 proofs in flight, not a handful: the data bound makes batches short, and short batches mean many of them are unsettled at once. Fleet sizing and the reward floor have to be read at that concurrency.

Proofs may finish out of order, but they cannot land out of order, because each accepted batch must start at max(lastLandedHeight + 1, resumeHeight). A finished proof for a later range waits until the earlier range has landed, and a range below the resume height can never be landed at all. This keeps the L1 checkpoint a single unbroken chain of contiguous ranges, permanently cut at every completed recovery.

The unsettled-depth cap

The unsettled depth at a height is the number of L2 blocks between the highest L1-accepted checkpoint and that height. The design caps it as a consensus rule: a correct validator must not prevote or precommit a block whose unsettled depth would exceed D_MAX − MARGIN_V (HALT-03), where

D_MAX   = floor((RETENTION_WINDOW - T_PROOF_MAX_PERMITTED - T_SETTLE_PIPELINE) / L2_BLOCK_INTERVAL)
MARGIN_V ≥ the number of L2 blocks produced during one Ethereum finality depth

There is exactly one enforcement point: the validators. The acceptance function must not reject an over-depth batch that is otherwise valid, because that could make a legitimately certified range permanently unlandable (L1-06).

The cap exists because retention is finite. Validators and full nodes are assumed to hold and serve unsettled data for a window; once a block’s data has aged out of that window and no proof exists, nobody can prove that range. So the protocol stops creating new unsettled blocks before that can happen. When the cap binds, production stops and the chain sits in a safe halt until provers catch up. That is a designed stop, not a failure of the protocol. If the settlement stall passes T_STALL, a permissionless, bonded, delayed recovery may replace the unsettled range above the latest accepted checkpoint — and it permanently retires the heights it discards, so the range it replaces can never be re-certified or landed again at any generation. It cannot reconstruct data at or below the checkpoint, so a halt over missing accepted data can still be permanent.

Notice what the cap is not: it does not limit proving latency and it is not misconduct for a proof to consume the whole 30-minute envelope. The envelope is the planning assumption; the cap sits above it.

Landing, finality and exit

When a proof exists, anyone may submit land(data, proof): one transaction carrying the range, the data (calldata and/or blobs) and the proof. The Inbox checks that the range starts at max(lastLandedHeight + 1, resumeHeight), derives the public inputs from its own state, verifies the proof, and only then writes the checkpoint record. There is no expiry, no allowlist and no pending record. The checkpoint carries the L2 height, block hash, state root, epoch, validator-set root, data commitment and the L1 block that accepted it. That transaction is the moment the data becomes canonical on Ethereum.

A bridge withdrawal then needs the checkpoint whose state root covers the signal, plus WITHDRAWAL_DELAY measured from that checkpoint’s l1BlockNumber; where the claim depends on an L1 fact that can still be reorganized, it additionally needs Ethereum finality (MSG-03). At 12-second Ethereum slots, two epochs is 64 slots, about 12.8 minutes — arithmetic on a sourced depth, to be re-derived if Ethereum’s configuration changes.

At the target data budget of about 131,072 bytes per L2 block, one block is one blob, so a batch cannot be longer than the blob limit of the single transaction that carries it together with its proof: six blocks at Ethereum’s target of six blobs per L1 block (786,432 bytes, exactly one L1 slot of blob capacity) and nine blocks at the hard maximum. Batch length is therefore set by data and by the atomic rule, not by preference — and because the batch is short, about 150 batches are unsettled inside the thirty-minute envelope, hence roughly 150 proofs in flight. That is arithmetic on sourced constants, not a measurement of this system.

The intervals add. Only the two-second cadence and the arithmetic are fixed today; the provers, the L1 inclusion path and the withdrawal delay are all waiting on measurement. What the design fixes is the order: production, then certification, then a proof, then one atomic landing, then Ethereum finality, then withdrawal.

What this gives you

  • Six separate clocks with names, so a latency claim can be checked instead of assumed.
  • A batch that cannot reach Ethereum half-settled: data and proof land in the same transaction.
  • A proving pipeline that overlaps production, so proving is not a stop-the-world step.
  • A cap that stops production before data can age out of the retention assumption, instead of backing up forever and stranding a certified range.
  • A batch length fixed by data and atomicity rather than by preference: one blob per block at the target rate, and one transaction for data plus proof, give at most about six blocks per batch.
  • An unambiguous moment — L1 acceptance — at which the data becomes public on Ethereum and withdrawals can begin to be counted.

What it does not give you

  • Two-second certification, two-second settlement or two-second withdrawals. The cadence is only the cadence.
  • A guarantee that a proof finishes inside 30 minutes. The envelope is a planning assumption; a slower proof is not misconduct, and the cap eventually binds.
  • Data availability on Ethereum during the proving window. That window’s availability comes from L2 participants and is an assumption, not an on-chain duty.
  • Any measured latency figure. L1 inclusion, the round budget, the retention window and the withdrawal delay are all not yet measured.
  • A small proving fleet or a long batch. About 150 batches are unsettled inside the thirty-minute envelope, so roughly 150 proofs must be able to be in flight at once; that is a consequence of the data bound, not a target that can be tuned away.

Check yourself

A user interface shows “confirmed” two seconds after submission. What has actually happened?

At most: the block was proposed, executed locally and voted on. It is not certified, and even a later confirmation above the latest checkpoint accepted on Ethereum would be provisional; its batch’s data is not on Ethereum and no proof exists. A display before acceptance must say that the confirmation is provisional and that the data and proof are not yet on Ethereum (STATUS-11).

The unsettled-depth cap binds. Why can a valid proof for that same deep range still land on L1?

Because the cap is a production rule enforced by validators, not an admission rule on land. Refusing a valid proof at L1 could strand a legitimately certified range, which the design forbids. The correct sequence is: production stops, existing ranges are proven and landed, and production resumes as the checkpoint advances.

Ten minutes after a block is certified, its proof is still running. Where is the block’s data?

On L2 participants’ machines. Validators and full nodes hold and serve it while the batch is unsettled. The canonical copy is created only when the accepting L1 transaction publishes the calldata or blobs. Retention until then is a stated assumption, not something the protocol enforces.

Why is a batch at most about six blocks at the target rate, rather than a larger number chosen for convenience?

Because the data and the proof must travel in one L1 transaction (L1-01), and the batch’s data rides in that transaction’s blobs. At the target rate one 2-second block is one 131,072-byte blob, and EIP-7691 allows 6 blobs per L1 block as the target and 9 as the maximum, so a 32-block batch would need about 32 blobs in one transaction — far past the ceiling. The bound is a consequence of the data and of the atomic rule, not a tuning choice, and it is why roughly 150 proofs are in flight rather than a handful.

Assumptions

  • Fewer than one third of each epoch’s voting power is malicious.
  • More than two thirds of the voting power is online and timely enough to complete rounds.
  • Validators and full nodes keep and serve unsettled data until the batch lands.
  • At least one adequately resourced prover finishes each batch; a normal proof consumes the envelope, it does not exceed the cap.
  • Ethereum includes valid transactions within a bounded delay and finalizes them.
  • The 30-minute envelope, the retention window and the withdrawal delay are planning values awaiting measurement.
  • Blob space is a market: the target rate is nominal, and sustained throughput is whatever the protocol’s fee budget buys at the prevailing blob price.
