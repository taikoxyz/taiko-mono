10 — Halt or roll back

Problem this lesson solves. It explains why keeping a PoS-finalized block permanent can require the chain to stop, what the unselected Mode B rollback would do and cost, and why a halt may be permanent when the data is gone.

In plain language

The promise. Mode A is the content of one rule: no contract function, client rule, timeout, validator rotation, admission rule, ordinary governance upgrade or operational procedure may change the canonical history at or below a height that reached STATUS-04 — PoS-finalized. It is stated as an absence of mechanisms plus positive obligations (REC-01). There is no history-replacement path, and one is not improvised when the chain is stuck.

Why stopping can be the only correct action. Progress can need something the rules cannot conjure: the data for a finalized or locked block (A-CONS-5), a quorum inside the timeout ladder (A-CONS-2), L1 facts that are Ethereum-final (SYS-02), the L1 set commitment for the next epoch (CONS-13), or an unsettled depth below the cap (HALT-03). When one fails and no rule-legal action can fix it, a correct validator stops signing and stops advancing its finalized view rather than choose between two histories (HALT-01). Halting is a correct outcome: the node keeps serving what it has and keeps its state to resume.

Restarting is not "starting fresh". Resume only from the highest finalized block whose data and certificate can be independently reconstructed; never adopt an alternative history; never treat an elapsed timeout as evidence that a certificate does not exist (STATUS-10). Restart rules add a durable signing-history check — a validator that cannot prove it has not already signed for a (height, round, type) must not sign, and amnesia is a signing fault, not a fresh start — plus replay rejection and a first act of data and certificate sync, not a proposal (HALT-02).

The cap that keeps a halt recoverable. Correct validators refuse to vote for a block that would push the unsettled depth past D_MAX. The cap is a consensus rule, not an operator policy, and it exists so the oldest unsettled batch never leaves the retrievability window before it can be proven and landed. A normal proof taking the whole D6 envelope — up to 30 minutes, i.e. 900 blocks at 2 s — is not misconduct and triggers no recovery action (HALT-03, DA-06). When the cap binds, production stops.

No rescue. No emergency key, guardian, DAO vote or operational procedure may be required to preserve Mode A, and none may be introduced to "resolve" a halt: no function can advance, rewind or replace the checkpoint, no DAO intervention is permitted for liveness, and no data-first publication may unblock settlement (HALT-04).

What Mode B would do — and why it is not selected. Mode B is the authorised fallback: permissionless L1 recovery of unsettled history (REC-02). Its only trigger is a settlement stall longer than T_STALL, derived from the D6 envelope plus witness generation, queueing, L1 inclusion and a margin. Anyone may invoke it by posting a bond; it takes effect only after T_RECOVERY_DELAY, and it is cancelled by acceptance of any valid batch extending the current checkpoint. The invoker chooses no checkpoint, no validator set and no configuration: recovery restores the last Ethereum-final L1 checkpoint and re-enters the epoch schedule there. A certificate for a discarded height is void canonically but remains slashing evidence; a late proof above the restored checkpoint is void; L2 transactions in discarded blocks are not replayed automatically; rollback depth is at most D_MAX blocks, conditionally on the cap being enforced and not as a bound on loss; user exposure is every L2 outcome above the checkpoint, with no universal bound on economic loss and no compensation from penalties (ECON-11).

Mode B is specified so the fallback is concrete, so the conditions that would make it necessary are enumerated, and so Mode A does not make it unimplementable. It is never described as satisfying Mode A's irreversible-finality promise: under Mode B the label is PoS-certified / provisional. It is not selected, and its selection is blocked until it is established that, inside the stated normal-operation assumptions, an ordinary malicious leader or a sub-threshold coalition cannot cheaply trigger a recovery that replaces honestly confirmed history. The trigger is a stall, and a stall can be induced or amplified by an outage; the delay and cancellation rules reduce but do not obviously remove the profitability of "force a stall, then roll back an unfavourable outcome". Inability to establish this is a blocker, not a disclosure item (REC-03).

The recorded dissent. An independent research stream asked to break Mode A concluded "Mode A is not feasible as stated", with the counterexample certified-but-unavailable data: a quorum signs block B whose data exists only in node memory; the holders vanish; under D5 no proof can be built; Mode A forbids discarding B, so settlement halts, possibly permanently. The lead agrees the counterexample is real and discloses it, but disagrees with the verdict: it is precisely a failure of the stated liveness assumptions — that correct validators hold and serve the data they voted for, and that at least one adequately-resourced prover exists (A-CONS-5, A-DA-2) — and D2 step 1 states that a safe halt outside the stated liveness assumptions is permitted and its existence alone is not evidence of infeasibility. Mode B removes the symptom, not the cause: it needs a new honest quorum and the L1 checkpoint's data, so if participation is permanently gone it restores nothing while adding the surface REC-03 blocks. The class already exists in the baseline (the June 2026 forced-inclusion void, an owner-only init3()), which shows it must be disclosed and mitigated, not that it is disqualifying. The disagreement is preserved (DECISIONS.md D-3, 04 — architecture decision §5). The falsifier is review question Q-A1: if a reviewer shows the counterexample is reachable inside the stated assumptions — that the availability premise is vacuous rather than conditional — the selection must be revisited under D2 step 2.

A halt can be permanent. The specification says so three times: "safe halt, possibly permanent unless a holder returns" (06 — required scenarios); "a halt, possibly permanent, if every holder disappears" (LIM-01); and clause (L3) of the liveness statement — "settlement halts, possibly permanently, unless a data holder returns" (LIVE-01). No rule may discard that finalized history to escape the halt.

Precise definitions

  • Mode A finality. A published commit certificate over (chain id, epoch, height, block hash) carrying strictly more than two thirds of the epoch's voting power, plus the receiver's own data-availability check; permanent in Mode A (STATUS-04).
  • No invalidation path. No mechanism may lower the checkpoint, replace a finalized block hash or discard finalized state; admission may not reject a batch extending the checkpoint; a certificate stays valid forever under its own epoch's set; upgrades must accept prior finalized history; no data-first path may be introduced (REC-01).
  • Safe halt. Stop signing and advancing rather than choose between histories or advance on unknown state, on any of: unobtainable finalized data, no quorum within the timeout ladder, non-final or disagreeing L1 facts, a missing L1 set commitment for the next epoch, or an exceeded unsettled-depth cap (HALT-01).
  • Restart. Resume only from the highest finalized block whose data and certificate can be independently reconstructed; never adopt an alternative history; enforce durable signing-history uniqueness; re-verify the epoch and set; reject replayed messages; sync before proposing (HALT-02).
  • Backpressure. The consensus-enforced unsettled-depth cap D_MAX, derived from the retention window and the block rate — a rule on production, never an admission condition on land (HALT-03).
  • No privileged rescue. No emergency operator, DAO rescue or data-first recovery (HALT-04).
  • Mode B, specified but not selected. Permissionless L1 recovery of unsettled history strictly above the last Ethereum-final L1 checkpoint, on an objective stall trigger, with a bond, a delay, cancellation by acceptance, fixed checkpoint/config boundaries, and late-evidence and replay rules (REC-02).
  • The Mode B blocker. Cheap-trigger resistance is unresolved, so Mode B may not be selected on this specification's analysis alone (REC-03); an elapsed timeout is never evidence of absence (STATUS-10), and penalties are not compensation (ECON-11).

Attacks this prevents

  • Two histories both claiming the irreversible guarantee. A validator that cannot resolve a conflict stops instead of committing to a branch (HALT-01, INV-01).
  • Timeout-as-absence. A certificate revealed later cannot invalidate a decision, because no rule drew a conclusion from silence (STATUS-10, WH-04).
  • The operator "unstick". No privileged lever can advance, rewind or replace the checkpoint, and the migration removes the baseline's init2()/init3()-style selectors (HALT-04, MIG-04).
  • Unbounded unsettled growth and unsafe restarts. The cap converts an unrecoverable aging failure into a bounded halt, and amnesia double-signing, replayed votes and branch adoption are each addressed by a named rule (HALT-02, HALT-03, DA-06).

Attacks this does not prevent

  • One third or more of Byzantine stake. Two conflicting certificates can both be valid and a ZK proof cannot detect it; the safety claims do not apply (F2, INV-01).
  • Permanent loss of the data. If every holder disappears no rule restores it and the halt may be permanent (LIVE-01, LIM-01).
  • Economic loss and malicious governance. Mode A protects history, not value: penalties are not compensation and no universal bound on loss is claimed (ECON-11); one upgrade can rewrite any rule, which is F2, outside the protocol's protection (GOV-02).
  • The availability counterexample and the Mode B blocker. The counterexample is real and disclosed; its reachability inside the stated assumptions is the open review question Q-A1, and the dissent is preserved (DECISIONS.md D-3). Mode B's cheap-trigger resistance may not be claimed: it is an open blocker (REC-03).

Self-check

A validator has seen no certificate for height H for an hour. May it conclude that none exists and vote for a conflicting block?

No. An elapsed timeout is never evidence of absence (STATUS-10). A correct validator keeps its lock, retries and accepts the certificate whenever it arrives; it must never finalize a conflicting block at that height, and it halts rather than choose (HALT-01).

Is a safe halt evidence that Mode A is infeasible?

No. D2 step 1 states that a safe halt outside the stated liveness assumptions is permitted and that its existence alone is not evidence of infeasibility (04 — architecture decision §5.2). The falsifier is different: a reviewer would have to show the availability counterexample is reachable inside the stated assumptions (Q-A1).

If Mode B were selected, would it undo a PoS-finalized block that was never landed?

It would replace history strictly above the last Ethereum-final L1 checkpoint: the certificate is void canonically but remains slashing evidence, L2 transactions in discarded blocks are not replayed automatically, and no universal bound on loss may be claimed (REC-02). That is exactly why it is not selected: it changes the guarantee class of an unsettled confirmation.

Challenge this. The lesson relied on: (i) A-CONS-1 — fewer than one third of each epoch's voting power is Byzantine; (ii) A-CONS-5 and A-DA-2 — somebody retains the data until a proof exists and at least one prover completes each batch; (iii) A-L1-1 — Ethereum includes valid transactions and does not reverse finality; (iv) the D2 procedure being followed, which is why Mode B may not be used on the strength of this specification alone; (v) D_MAX, the retention window and every Mode B timing term being unmeasured placeholders; (vi) the lead's reading of the availability counterexample as a liveness failure — a reading the recorded dissent disputes, with Q-A1 open; (vii) A-GOV-1, the assumption that the DAO does not execute a malicious upgrade. Falsifier: find a sentence in this lesson that bounds the duration of a halt, presents Mode B as safe, or calls the dissent resolved.
