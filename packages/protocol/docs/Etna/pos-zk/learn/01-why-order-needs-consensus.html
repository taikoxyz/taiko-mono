01 — Why a blockchain needs someone to decide the order

Problem this lesson solves. It explains who decides the order of transactions on this L2, and why that is a consensus question about stake and agreement rather than a coding question about whether a block is valid.

In plain language

A rollup is a chain that executes transactions away from Ethereum and then commits the result back to Ethereum. The specification's glossary calls a batch a contiguous range of L2 blocks accepted on L1 by one transaction that carries both the batch's data and its proof. glossary · D5

Order decides outcomes. A nonce must follow the nonce before it; a swap executes at the price left by the trades ahead of it; a liquidation succeeds or fails depending on whether it lands before or after the borrower's repayment. Feed the same transactions to the same rules in two different orders and you get two different states — two different answers to "what happened to me".

Execution validity is a statement about arithmetic and rules: if you run this block from its parent, does it produce the claimed result and pass every rule? Anyone can check that. Agreement is a different statement: which of the possible histories is the one that counts? Two different blocks at the same height can both be execution-valid. A checker that says "yes" to both has not chosen between them, and no amount of code can choose, because a deterministic function applied to two different inputs correctly returns two different answers. What chooses is a consensus protocol: a rule that lets a defined set of participants pick one block per height and makes picking two expensive. That expense is stake that can be taken away (INV-03, MEM-06).

In this design the chooser is the L2 PoS validator set: permissionless validators who bond TAIKO and vote on L2 blocks. The fixed decision that permits this is D4, which says L2 PoS determines binding transaction order (D4). The roles of Ethereum are stated in SYS-01: data availability, proof verification, settlement and bridge anchoring. Ethereum proposers do not order L2 transactions here.

That last sentence is an obligation, not an opinion. GEN-09 fixes the name of this architecture and forbids describing it with the term reserved for a design where L1 proposers do the ordering. The reason is blunt: a user who believes Ethereum ordered their transaction believes something about who could have reordered it that is not true here. The course map puts the same warning on its first page. Naming is a security-relevant interface because users act on what the name implies.

Precise definitions

  • Rollup. A chain whose execution happens off Ethereum and whose data and result are committed to Ethereum. The binding form here is fixed by D5: data and a valid proof in one L1 transaction (D5).
  • Batch. A contiguous L2 block range accepted on L1 by one transaction carrying its data and its proof; the single atomic acceptance path is L1-01 (glossary).
  • Sequencing authority. Whoever fixes the binding order of L2 transactions. Here it is L2 PoS, by D4 (D4).
  • Based rollup. The term for a design in which L1 proposers determine L2 order. GEN-09 forbids applying it to this design, and LIM-02 records based sequencing as a rejected alternative.
  • Execution validity. A node executed the block against a known parent state and every consensus and validity rule passed. The specification makes this a status with a local guarantee only: the node will not build on an invalid block (STATUS-02).
  • Consensus finality. The point at which a block is settled into one history. Its form here is a published commit certificate over (chain id, epoch, height, block hash) carrying strictly more than two thirds of the epoch's voting power, plus the receiver's own data check (STATUS-04, CONS-03).
  • Safety threshold. The safety argument holds only while fewer than one third of the relevant voting power is Byzantine — an assumption, not a measured fact (A-CONS-1, INV-01).
  • Cadence. The production interval between L2 proposals, fixed at 2 s by D1. It is not finality, proof, settlement or withdrawal latency (SYS-03).

Read the factor of 6 the right way: it says the design puts many more ordering decisions per day into L2 than an L1-proposer schedule would, and each of those decisions is made by whoever the L2 leader-selection rule picks for that height (CONS-06). It does not say anything about how many independent operators exist, how concentrated the stake is, or how fast finality arrives — those are unmeasured or assumed, and later lessons keep them separate.

Attacks this prevents

  • Expectation misattribution. A user or reviewer treating this chain as if Ethereum proposers ordered it, and therefore assuming an L1-sequencing guarantee that does not exist — the failure mode GEN-09 names.
  • "Valid, therefore mine is history." Using local execution validity as evidence of a position in the canonical history, when validity is a local status and finality comes from a certificate (STATUS-02, CONS-12).
  • Silent authority drift. A document or interface that leaves the ordering authority unstated, so that the one-third assumption behind every finality claim becomes invisible to the user relying on it.

Attacks this does not prevent

  • Reordering or censorship by whoever is selected to propose, inside the rules. This lesson names the authority; the constraints live in the consensus and forced-inclusion rules (CONS-01, FI-01–FI-05).
  • A coalition holding one third or more of the relevant stake. The safety claim does not apply there, and the specification says so rather than claiming protection (A-CONS-1, INV-01).
  • L1 censorship of data, proofs or forced-inclusion transactions (T-7).
  • Any implementation defect: this is a specification-level design, with no implementation and no measurement (GEN-01).
  • The gap between an honest name and what a reader actually reads.

Self-check

Two blocks at the same height are both execution-valid. Has the protocol decided which one counts?

No. Execution validity is local and per-block (STATUS-02). Deciding which history counts is what a commit certificate does, and the safety argument for uniqueness is INV-01 under its stated premises. If both blocks also gathered valid certificates, the premises have failed and the specification's honest answer is detection and disclosure, not a stronger claim.

Where is transaction order decided in this design, and what does Ethereum do instead?

L2 PoS decides binding order (D4). Ethereum provides data availability, proof verification, settlement and bridge anchoring, and is not the sequencer (SYS-01, GEN-09).

Is the 2 s figure a finality time?

No. It is the block production cadence fixed by D1. Finality, proof, L1 acceptance, Ethereum finality and withdrawal are five further timelines, and SYS-03 forbids equating any two of them (SYS-03).

Challenge this. The lesson relied on: (i) the specification's use of the term "based rollup" in GEN-09 as the definition, not a colloquial one; (ii) the claim that ordering, not validity, is the property a user's safety actually depends on; (iii) the 2 s cadence and the 43,200-blocks-a-day figure being design arithmetic from D1, not measurements; (iv) the 12 s L1 slot being a sourced external input that no normative rule may depend on (GEN-06); (v) the reader accepting that "order is a consensus problem" also imports the one-third assumption it carries. Falsifier: find a sentence in this lesson that implies Ethereum proposers order L2 transactions — that would be a defect under GEN-09 and should be reported.
