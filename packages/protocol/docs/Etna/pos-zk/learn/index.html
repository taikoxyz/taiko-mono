Etna PoS + ZK — an engineer’s course

A layer-2 chain whose ordering is decided by its own permissionless proof-of-stake validators, whose blocks are settled on Ethereum by zero-knowledge proofs, and whose batch data is published to Ethereum and bound to its proof. Eleven lessons and two reference pages, written for people who have to build, operate or integrate against it and arranged to be read in order.

This is a design, not a running system. Nothing described here is deployed, audited or measured. Where a number is not yet measured it is labelled unmeasured; where it follows from arithmetic it is labelled derived; where it comes from a specification or a published standard it is labelled sourced. The authoritative rules — with identifiers, edge cases and exact encodings — are in the specification; this course explains the same design in plain language.

The design in five sentences

  1. Validators order the chain. Anyone who bonds the TAIKO token on Ethereum can join the validator set, the set for each epoch is fixed on Ethereum before the epoch starts, and staying selectable for future versions is renewed by a periodic L1 heartbeat.
  2. Blocks come every two seconds and are PoS-certified once more than two thirds of the epoch’s voting power has voted for them — normally a few rounds later. That confirmation stays provisional until the batch is accepted on Ethereum.
  3. A batch lands on Ethereum only with its proof. One Ethereum transaction carries a zero-knowledge proof of both the consensus certificate and correct execution, together with the batch data or a reference to a publication that already holds it, or the batch is not accepted at all.
  4. Proving takes minutes, not seconds. Up to about thirty minutes is normal, so the chain keeps producing and certifying while proofs catch up.
  5. A settled checkpoint is never rewritten, and everything above it is provisional. The one rule that acts on the provisional range is the stall resolution: the DAO may queue a single entry during a settlement stall, and after its stored deadline anyone may execute it once to resume settlement without erasing that range — it invalidates a superseded certificate only as a batch’s head evidence, and the range stays valid history that a later batch can extend, while its signals must be resubmitted. Nothing obliges governance to act, so a stall it does not act on halts the chain; value at or below the last accepted checkpoint still exits on Ethereum alone through a withdrawal root of k distinct-family attestations (funded proving work over data that is still retrievable).

Every duration, rate and size above is a design input or a planning envelope, not a measurement; the pages below label what is derived and what is unmeasured.

How to read this course

The pages are written to be read one after another. Each one states what it establishes, uses only terms the pages before it have already introduced, and stands on its own if you stop there.

  1. What Etna PoS + ZK is — the shape of the system: who does what, what Ethereum still provides, and the five questions people mix up. It gives you the picture the rest of the course uses.
  2. The life of a transaction — one transaction end to end, and the eight statuses you will see in tooling — including the provisional PoS-certified status — with what each status actually guarantees and what it does not.
  3. How the chain agrees — proposals, votes, the two-thirds quorum, locks, commit certificates, proposer selection and view changes: how an L2 block becomes certified in the first place.
  4. Stake, validators and epochs — bonding TAIKO, joining an epoch’s validator set, the L1 heartbeat that renews eligibility, how voting power is counted, exits, and how a new node starts safely.
  5. What the proof proves — the zero-knowledge statement: the consensus certificate, execution, the data binding, the configuration it binds and the signed recovery generation that scopes certificates — and what it deliberately does not prove.
  6. Data first, proof binds — one accepting transaction binds the data and the proof: the data carried or a live publication record referenced, the calldata and blob paths, the blob binding, the per-epoch configuration registry, and what a failed verification does.
  7. Two seconds and thirty minutes — block cadence, certification, proving, L1 acceptance, Ethereum finality and withdrawals on one timeline, including the data bound on batch length and the proofs in flight it implies.
  8. When things go wrong — withholding, missing certificates, offline validators, lost data and settlement stalls: how a stall halts the chain safely, keeps every accepted checkpoint untouched, leaves the range above it as valid history whose signals must be resubmitted, and how the timelocked, resume-only stall resolution clears it when governance acts.
  9. Censorship and the bridge — what censorship resistance this design does and does not provide: statistical resistance for unpublished data, no remedy against isolation and no general inclusion list, plus how bridge messages are authenticated and what a withdrawal root requires.
  10. Who pays, who earns, who loses — validator costs and rewards, what a slashable offence needs, penalties, unbonding, and how minimum stake is derived.
  11. Forced inclusion — the one inclusion obligation: a frontier that must advance past the oldest pending publication, resolving it dead first, then on the live-only void grounds, then by walking its transactions in their own order and running or setting aside each at its turn; enforcement inside the proof and never at the door; and the honest bound, exclusion per unit of the censor’s L1 spending rather than a latency guarantee.
  12. Glossary — every term the course uses, in one table, with the meaning the design fixes for it. It is a reference: open it whenever a word needs pinning down, and each entry stands alone.
  13. What this design does not promise — the honest edge of the whole course: a design and not an implementation, provisional confirmations, unbounded off-chain loss, a stall that halts safely and is cleared only by the timelocked stall resolution when governance acts, no protocol-level liveness guarantee and no permissionless recovery, and the guarantees the design deliberately does not make.

A note on honesty. Every lesson states what the design guarantees and, just as carefully, what it does not. Where a guarantee rests on an assumption — most rest on fewer than a third of stake being malicious, on enough validators being online, and on somebody keeping the data until the proof lands — the lesson says so in an Assumptions box. If this course ever claims more than the specification does, treat that as a bug in the course.
