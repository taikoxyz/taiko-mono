02 — Five things people confuse

Problem this lesson solves. It separates the five questions that a phrase like "my transaction is confirmed" collapses into one — execution validity, consensus agreement, data availability, economic accountability and Ethereum settlement — so that every later lesson can name which one it means.

In plain language

Five questions are usually asked as one:

  1. Is this block execution-valid? Did running it produce the claimed result and pass the rules? Anyone who re-executes it can answer, alone.
  2. Is it agreed? Have the participants who are empowered to choose the history signed this block into it? In this design that means the L2 validator set and its commit certificate. The selected consensus family is Tendermint/CometBFT-class BFT with single-slot finality (04 — architecture decision §1, sourced design decision).
  3. Is the data available? Can anyone who needs the block's bytes actually obtain them — not "did I receive them", but "are they retrievable". The specification draws the line at STATUS-09: a node holding a block may not treat its own possession as availability for anyone else.
  4. Is anyone economically accountable? If a participant breaks a rule, is there an objective, self-contained, on-chain-verifiable offence and stake that can be taken for it (WH-02, ECON-04)?
  5. Is it settled on Ethereum? Has an L1 transaction accepted the batch's data and a valid proof together, and is that transaction itself at the L1 finality the design requires (STATUS-06, STATUS-07)?

One more separation holds the other five apart. SYS-03 lists six distinct timelines — block cadence T1, PoS finality latency T2, proof latency T3, L1 acceptance T4, Ethereum finality T5 and withdrawal availability T6 — and forbids any rule, parameter, interface or status display from equating two of them. A 2 s cadence is a production interval. It is not agreement, not availability, not settlement and not an exit.

Why people collapse the five: each one, taken alone, sounds like "the transaction happened". Availability feels like a fact about the network rather than a duty somebody has to keep (LIM-01). Settlement feels like a formality once a proof exists. Economic accountability feels like a guarantee rather than a conditional argument about prices (A-ECO-1). The five are separate because they can fail separately: a block can be execution-valid on your node and unavailable to everyone else; it can be PoS-finalized and never settle; it can be settled on L1 and later revoked by an L1 reorganisation below finality (STATUS-06).

Precise definitions

  • Execution validity. This node executed the block against a known parent state and every consensus and validity rule passed. Guarantee: this node will not build on an invalid block. Revocable if the parent is displaced; visible to this node only (STATUS-02).
  • Agreement (voted / PoS-finalized). A vote is one validator's signed commitment, irreversible for that validator and constraining through the lock rule (STATUS-03, CONS-04). Finality is a published commit certificate over (chain id, epoch, height, block hash) with strictly more than two thirds of the epoch's voting power, plus the receiver's own data check (STATUS-04).
  • Data availability. The property that the data can be obtained by those who need it. Canonical availability for a settled batch is the accepting L1 transaction (DA-01); before that it is a retention duty, not a cryptographic property (LIM-01), and local possession is not availability (STATUS-09).
  • Economic accountability. Stake that is bonded and active can be slashed for objectively evidenced offences, and slashing follows the stake held at the time of the offence (INV-03, MEM-06). The scope of user losses — what is and is not compensated — is its own rule (ECON-11).
  • Ethereum settlement. The Inbox accepted data and proof in one transaction and advanced its checkpoint; then the accepting transaction reaches Ethereum finality (L1-01, STATUS-06, STATUS-07).
  • Cadence. T1, fixed at 2 s by D1 under stated operating assumptions; none of the other four questions is answered by it (SYS-03).

Two arithmetic illustrations, kept honest about their inputs. First, the quorum threshold is the minimal integer q with 3q > 2 · total (CONS-03). With an illustrative total of 300 units — the specification fixes no such total — the threshold is 201 (derived: 3 × 200 = 600 is not greater, 3 × 201 = 603 is). The specification's own numbers are the formula and the tags; the 300 is the course's illustration and is unmeasured in the design. Second, 30 minutes is a planning envelope of 1,800 s (derived: D6; T_PROOF_ENVELOPE in 09 is tagged decided and explicitly not a worst-case bound), and at 2 s that is 900 L2 blocks (derived, PARAM-02). A proof that takes longer is not misconduct and triggers no recovery action (HALT-03).

Attacks this prevents

  • Premature value transfer. Treating an inclusion, a vote, or even PoS finality as settlement while the batch's data and proof are not on Ethereum — the failure mode STATUS-11 exists to prevent.
  • Availability inference. Concluding from one node's possession that the data is retrievable by others (STATUS-09), which hides the certified-but-unavailable failure class until a halt.
  • Proof-as-settlement. Treating proof-ready as accepted on L1, and therefore withdrawing early (STATUS-05 vs STATUS-06).
  • Timeout-as-absence. Concluding that a certificate, proof or block does not exist because a timer expired (STATUS-10); timeouts trigger local actions, never assertions about what exists elsewhere.

Attacks this does not prevent

  • A safety failure: one third or more of the relevant stake is Byzantine, in which case two conflicting certificates can both verify and a ZK proof cannot detect it (A-CONS-1, INV-01).
  • Data unavailability for a finalized block: settlement may halt, possibly permanently, if every holder disappears. The specification discloses this and calls a safe halt the correct outcome (LIM-01, HALT-01).
  • An L1 reorganisation below the finality depth, which can revoke accepted-on-L1 (STATUS-06).
  • Ordering and MEV extraction by whoever may propose within the rules.
  • Compensation of user losses: the specification states the scope of what is not compensated (ECON-11).

Self-check

A block is locally execution-valid on my node. What have I learned about what other nodes think?

Only that this node will not build on an invalid block (STATUS-02). You have learned nothing about agreement, and nothing about whether others can obtain the data (STATUS-09).

The batch is proof-ready. May I withdraw?

No. Withdrawal requires accepted on L1 or Ethereum-finalized, plus the message-authentication path and the delay in MSG-03 (STATUS-08). Proof-ready means only that the batch can be landed (STATUS-05).

Which of the five questions does a "2 s" progress bar answer?

None. Cadence is separated from every other latency (SYS-03), and any display before accepted on L1 must state that the batch's data and proof are not yet on Ethereum (STATUS-11).

Challenge this. The lesson relied on: (i) the five-question split being a teaching device, while the specification's own separation is the six timelines of SYS-03 — the mapping between them is the course's, not a normative taxonomy; (ii) PoS-finalized being read together with INV-01's premises, including the handoff premise that the assurance page marks as an assumed-and-argued review target; (iii) the "30 minutes" being an envelope fixed by D6 and tagged as a planning assumption, never a bound; (iv) Ethereum finality of about two epochs (≈ 12.8 min) being a sourced property of Ethereum's current configuration, tagged in 09 as depending on that configuration, with F_L1 unmeasured; (v) the availability of a finalized block being a retention duty, not a property, before L1 acceptance (LIM-01). Falsifier: find a cell in the worked table that asserts a guarantee stronger than the linked status rule — that is a defect, because the specification wins.
