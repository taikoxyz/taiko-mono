## F1 — Bonded withholding can earn the challenge deposit (High design gap; exploitable when a holder controls the needed fragment)

**Preconditions.** A publisher or custodian has a live 10-TAIKO `DutyClaim` for a fragment that is not yet publicly staged or revealed; a user or observer needs the bytes and can fund the challenge. This is a service-availability attack on provisional data, not a claim that a soft block should be irreversible.

1. The holder signs an authentic fragment claim and selectively declines P2P requests. A relying peer lacking the bytes correctly reports `UNKNOWN`; it cannot locally validate or prove that branch.
2. The peer opens `challengeAvailability`, locking `Q = 2 × 4,000,000 × (basefee + 2 gwei)` ETH-wei. The holder retains the bytes until near the 600-second response deadline.
3. The holder (or its relay) publishes the exact fragment in `respondAvailability`. The response earns **all** `Q` and leaves its 10-TAIKO reserve intact. At the page's 20-gwei example, `Q=0.176 ETH`; if response gas costs below `Q`, delaying service is profitable. If no one pays to challenge, the same refusal has no objective default. Repeating with fresh positions repeats the option.

**Cost and gain.** The 10-TAIKO reserve is refundable if the holder answers; there is transaction gas and opportunity cost. Gain is the difference between `Q` and response cost, plus any external value of a delayed/unknown provisional branch. The exploit needs no forged signature, invalid ZK proof, L1 censorship, or economic-identity oracle. It does not imply that the holder can force an invalid canonical root.

**Gate and failed defense.** R6 requires an active-holder withholding defense with objective penalties. The service rule defines a response to a *paid* challenge, and expressly permits another party to satisfy it; it never makes original P2P refusal or delayed publication of an early signed block a slashable deadline. The separate absolute `PublicationClaim` is optional and exists only once a complete segment context is sealed, so a holder can issue early bonded fragment claims without signing it. The 360-second stage gate protects **canonical** first disclosure and does not penalize this pre-stage service behavior. See `design/accountability.html:16` (`#challenge`), `:20` (`#sealed-publication`), `:30` (`#offenses`), `:160` (selective serving limit); `design/roles.html:53,85`; `design/staging.html:7–13` (`#purpose`).

**Minimum fix.** Give any early block displayed as bonded an objective, time-bounded publication duty for its already-known exact block record/fragments. Specify a permissionless L1 publication receipt or bounded proof tying those bytes to the signed fragment manifest, an absolute deadline, and a slash that late paid challenge responses cannot cure. Keep the later sealed-context covenant for the final DA mode. If that cost is unsuitable, explicitly limit the early label to paid-on-challenge retrieval and adjudicate whether that narrower service satisfies R6; do not present it as active-holder withholding deterrence.

## F2 — The stated anti-monopoly costs do not attach to ordinary canonical capture (Medium specification gap; exploitable with sustained prover/builder capital)

**Preconditions.** A well-funded actor can stage, prove and buy L1 inclusion for valid segments repeatedly. Outsiders remain technically permitted to stage and submit. No identity linkage or equal market-share target is assumed.

1. The actor pre-stages valid candidates with its own coinbase, prepares both proofs, and routes the mature submission through its preferred L1 builders. It can keep winning the L1 race while excluding discretionary transactions for which users do not pay the separate force-queue fee.
2. It need not open a publisher bucket, accept a proof job, or breach any signed service promise. It pays the normal stage/landing/proving costs but no penalty or increasing price for holding all canonical ordering. It collects the chosen coinbase's L2 fees and any ordering value.
3. An outsider can create a different stage and proof, but pays duplicate DA and proof cost, waits the same 360 seconds, and receives no priority. Reusing the actor's stage does not redirect the staged coinbase. A user can force a bounded transaction, but that shifts a fee to the censored user and does not contest control of discretionary ordering.

**Cost and gain.** Normal L1 gas, twice-published DA, proof hardware, and builder payments versus all won block fees and extractable ordering value. The 600-TAIKO bucket and 1,000-TAIKO job bond are avoided; the 50% force-fee sink is paid only when a user or spammer enqueues, not by the actor for winning ordinary segments. This is an economic concentration channel, not an exclusive on-chain admission or a guaranteed ability to beat every funded outsider.

**Gate and failed defense.** R6 calls for concrete anti-monopoly economics, not guaranteed equal shares. Zero exclusive leases and linear collateral for *voluntary promises* keep entry open but do not change the marginal economics of repeatedly owning canonical order. The design itself leaves strong deterrence open. See `design/roles.html:51,208,215–223` (`#competition`), `design/codec.html:49,97` (staged coinbase and allocation), `design/index.html:170–179` (`#forced`), `design/staging.html:7–13` (age and nonexclusive stage), `design/arguments.html:119,132`.

**Minimum fix.** Specify an identity-independent economic mechanism aimed at repeat control of ordinary ordering, or provide a bounded cost/gain model demonstrating that the existing mechanisms deter that precise tactic under named inclusion, fee and MEV assumptions. Tabulate why rotation, caps, auctions and decay are selected or rejected for that tactic. A per-address cap or assertion that market share will equalize would not close it.

## Traces that did not establish another finding

- A private body first disclosed in `submitSegment` has no 360-second-old actual-data stage, so the stated admission checks reject it. Earlier nonexclusive stage publication is allowed by literal R7.
- Re-salting an already published context cannot erase `getFirstPublication` or cure a missed signed sealed-publication deadline; the immutable context receipt and first-publication lookup cover that case (`staging.html:97–116`; `accountability.html:20`).
- Naming a victim as fee beneficiary or producer without its valid exact statement authorization fails the stated producer check; it does not create conflict-slashing evidence (`staging.html:123–158`; `accountability.html:21–29`).
- Self-reporting one slashed 10-TAIKO position returns at most 1 TAIKO and sinks 9; self-processing a force request still sinks half its fee. Neither is a free token mint (`roles.html:116–122,218–219`).
- Two capable provers can race without a service ticket; a losing ordinary transaction may still pay gas. No fairness or guaranteed winner was inferred (`index.html:148–164`; `roles.html:13–19`).

## Limits

This is a design-rule review of the named files at the named commit. The fixture documents give isolated encoding examples, not a valid execution segment, signature, proof or production benchmark. I did not independently recompute their Keccak values. I did not inspect linked legacy source, deploy a contract, observe live L1 state, or test performance. Sound proof production, honest funded inclusion, recoverable archive data and nonmalicious DAO upgrades are explicit assumptions; zero capable provers is outside the claimed liveness guarantee. Findings F1–F2 concern normative scope and incentives, not hypothetical bugs in code that does not yet exist.
