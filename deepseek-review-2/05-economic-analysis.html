Does This Make Economic Sense?

Aspect 5 of the DeepSeek independent adversarial review of PR #22034 (v14 design). Review date 2026-08-21. Companions: index · soundness · attacks · auction sims · L2 vs L1 timestamps · doc consistency · model checker.

Verdict: yes — economically coherent, with three honest caveats the design itself half-states. The mechanism is a standard revenue-maximizing auction over the sequencing rent, with unusually robust fallbacks (the chain earns nothing and still lives with 0 bidders; forced inclusion is priced at cost). The economics make sense as market design. The three caveats: (1) the single-bidder equilibrium hands ~95% of the rent to a reserve-price monopolist, so the reserve floor — not the auction — is the actual price-setting mechanism when competition is thin (accepted, but the consequence is understated); (2) every deterrence claim that matters is denominated in a governance-estimated "per-epoch MEV bound" — with no oracle by directive, the entire safety economics rests on a guess; (3) the equivocation sizing only deters at watchtower probability 1 (quantified below), and no user restitution means the market, not the protocol, must price the residual trust.

5.2 Rent dissipation and the thin-market problem

Simulation (aspect 3): with N bidders the price converges to the second-highest estimate (rent partially dissipated, DAO captures it — if row 1 is treasury). With 1 bidder the price is the reserve floor: at illustrative values the monopolist pays 0.010 vs a mean seat value of 0.20 ETH/epoch and keeps ~95% of the rent, renewing at reserve every T_max. Two structural consequences:

  • The reserve floor is the real price in thin markets. "Permissionless entry" is then the only check on monopoly rents, and the reserve must be set near the expected seat value — which requires the same MEV estimate as L_safety (5.3). Governance sets both; both are guesses.
  • Winner's-curse churn (overbid → quit after q epochs) creates service oscillation: 25–50% of simulated tenures end in early quit. Real-world consequence: preconf service reliability suffers exactly when competition is fiercest. A funding bond (3.4 of the auction report) would damp this; none is specified.

5.3 Deterrence economics: the watchtower gap, quantified

Equivocation payoff = stolen MEV − L_safety (paid only if caught; no clawback of the stolen value; no restitution by design). Expected profit = Λ·MEV − p·L_safety. The design sizes L_safety ≥ Λ·MEV:

The preconf-vs-record variant is watchtower-dependent by design (r11-N2 admits this qualitatively), but the sizing formula silently assumes p = 1. Since the double-EBC variant (the only self-materializing one) is also the cheapest to avoid, a rational equivocator uses preconf-vs-record and bets on p < 1. Either (a) size L_safety ≥ Λ·MEV/p_target for an explicit p_target, or (b) adopt the bonded-accusation seat suspension (§13-T.12) to collapse the exposure window to detection latency. As written, the ETH-denominated safety bond — the design's central theft deterrent — is quantitatively undersized at any realistic p.

5.4 The L_safety estimation problem (the deeper issue)

I9 forbids oracles, so "Λ × per-epoch extractable MEV" must be a governance-estimated constant. But per-epoch MEV is not an observable on-chain quantity; it depends on L2 flow, CEX-DEX spreads, and liquidation volume — it can change by an order of magnitude across a tenure (the doc admits the spike residual and re-prices at T_max). So the design's central safety guarantee ("matched at the sizing epoch") is exactly as good as a committee's MEV forecast, re-made every T_max. The design is honest about the re-pricing cadence; it is not honest that the estimate itself is the load-bearing input — if governance under-forecasts, equivocation is profitable in expectation even at p = 1 during high-MEV epochs. Recommend: express L_safety as a governance parameter with an explicit forecasting procedure and a spike-inclusive worst-case bound (§13-S.4), or accept the §13-T.12 suspension as the primary deterrent.

5.6 Economic bottom line

The redesign is economically sensible as a market design: it prices the sequencing monopoly, funds its own fallbacks, and keeps the chain alive at zero revenue. Its economic weaknesses are not structural but informational: three governance-guessed quantities (reserve floor, L_safety/MEV bound, freshness ceiling) carry the entire incentive structure, the watchtower-probability gap undersizes the central theft deterrent, and the seat price's destination is unspecified. Fix those four, and the economics close.
